# The Line of Women
### A tale of Duchess Eleonore and Rowan

---

## Prologue — The Dream in the Mist

Before she ever saw her face, Eleonore had already followed her once, in a country that existed only behind closed eyes.

In the dream, the forest was always the same: birches pale as bone, mist pooling ankle-deep between the roots, the hush of a world holding its breath. Eleonore walked barefoot through it without cold, without fear, drawn forward by a silhouette that moved always a little ahead of her — a woman's shape, dark-haired, unhurried, never quite turning around. She could not have said how she knew it was a woman, or why her chest ached at the sight of that unturning back, only that some part of her recognized the shape the way one recognizes a voice calling one's own name from another room.

She never caught her. She never once saw her face. But each time the dream came, she woke with the same words trembling on her lips, spoken to no one: *wait for me.*

On the morning this story begins, she woke with those words still warm in her mouth, her heart beating as though she had run some great distance, though she had not moved from her bed. Outside her window, the sky over Castle Albrecht was the pale grey of a held breath, dawn not yet decided whether to break clear or grey.

She rose, lit her candle though the room was already growing light, and opened the small book in which she kept the things she did not say aloud.

*I dreamed of her again,* she wrote. *I do not know her name. I do not know that she exists at all outside of sleep. And yet I woke this morning surer than I have ever been of anything at court: that somewhere, she is real, and that I am already walking toward her.*

She closed the book before she could read her own words back and doubt them. Then she rang for her attendants, and the Duchess of Albrecht became, once more, herself.

## Chapter One — The Lily Among Thorns

**I. Morning Light at Castle Albrecht**

Dawn came to Castle Albrecht the way it came to everything old and well-defended: slowly, and from a great height. The light found the eastern towers first, gilding the weathercocks before it ever reached the courtyard below, so that for a quarter of an hour each morning the castle seemed to wear a crown it took off again by breakfast.

In her chamber, Eleonore lay a while longer than she needed to, listening to the castle wake beneath her — the far-off clatter of the kitchens, a dog barking twice in the stable yard, the particular creak of the third step on the servants' stair that no amount of oil had ever silenced. These sounds were as familiar to her as her own pulse, and as little hers. She was mistress of all of it and author of none of it; the castle ran on rhythms laid down before her marriage and would run on them after, whatever she did or failed to do within its walls.

She thought, not for the first time, that there was a particular loneliness in being loved by an entire household and known by none of it.

**II. A Duchess's Duties**

Her attendants came at the customary hour, and the customary transformation began: the plain linen of sleep exchanged for the blue wool that suited a cool morning, her hair braided and pinned with the quiet efficiency of women who had done this every day for years and would do it every day for years more. Eleonore sat through it as she always did, offering the small kindnesses that cost her nothing — a question about a sister's wedding, a compliment on a new ribbon — because it seemed to her the least a duchess could give in exchange for so many hands.

She broke her fast with Edward in the small dining hall he preferred to the great one, both of them still soft with sleep, a single candle burning low between them though the windows were bright enough without it.

**Edward** *(not looking up from a letter)*: "You slept poorly. I heard you moving before the bell."

**Eleonore**: "A dream, nothing more. It has already left me."

**Edward** *(a brief, genuine glance, then back to the letter)*: "See that it doesn't linger. You've the harvest accounts to review with the steward, and I'd have your opinion on them before I give mine to the King's man."

**Eleonore** *(a small, practiced smile)*: "You always say you want my opinion before you've already decided what it will be."

**Edward** *(the ghost of a smile in return, rare and real)*: "Because you are usually right, and it saves me the trouble of admitting it twice."

*It was, Eleonore thought, as close as they came to tenderness most mornings — a kind of respect worn smooth and comfortable as an old glove, warm enough to live in, never quite warm enough to dream in. She did not resent it. She had married a fair man who kept his word, and she had seen enough of the alternative in other women's marriages to know what a mercy that was. But mercy, she had learned, and warmth were not always the same coin.*

**III. The Castle in Motion**

She left him to his letters and walked the long gallery toward the steward's office, and the castle moved around her the way a river moves around a stone that has always been there. Servants bowed and continued about their errands; a pair of hounds skidded past in pursuit of something only they could see; two of the King's men, dust still on their boots from the northern road, stood in low conversation near the map room, and fell silent as she passed, which told her more than their words would have.

She had seen the maps herself, once, spread across Edward's table in the candlelight while he thought she was asleep: the northern border marked in red ink where the marcher lords beyond the Greywater had grown bold, the King's demands for men and grain written in a hand grown increasingly impatient. War was a word no one said aloud in the halls of Albrecht, the way one does not say the name of an illness one hopes will pass if left unspoken. But it moved through the castle all the same, in the tightness around Edward's eyes, in the extra guards at the gate, in the way the King's men no longer smiled when they bowed to her.

She thought: *the world is arranging itself into something none of us chose,* and did not yet know how true that thought would prove.

**IV. The Gardens and the Distant Figure**

It was in search of air, more than duty, that she came out onto the south terrace an hour later, the accounts finished, her morning's obligations discharged. Below her, the gardens rolled away toward the orchard wall in their late-spring green, and it was here — only here — that the castle's weight seemed to lift from her shoulders.

She was not looking for anything in particular when she saw the figure among the herb beds: a woman, dark-haired, sleeves pushed back, moving between the rows with an unhurried certainty that belonged entirely to herself, as though she answered to no bell and no household and no husband. Too far to call to. Too far to see her face clearly. And yet something in the set of her shoulders, in the private, listening way she paused over a plant before touching it, struck a chord in Eleonore that she could not name — like the first bars of a song she had once known by heart and had long since forgotten she knew.

She stood at the balustrade longer than she meant to, watching a stranger tend the earth, and felt, absurdly, as though she were seeing someone she had already met.

*It is the dream,* she told herself, and did not believe it, and could not stop thinking of it either.

**V. Evening Reflection**

That night, before the candle guttered, she took out her small book again.

*I saw a woman in the garden today,* she wrote, *tending the herbs, too far off to know her face. I do not know why I could not look away. I do not know why, of all the faces in this castle, hers is the one I carried up to my chamber with me, like a stone in my pocket I keep turning over.*

*Perhaps it is nothing. Perhaps it is only that I dreamed of a shape in the mist, and my heart, foolish and hungry as it is, has decided to fit that shape to the first stranger who crossed my sight.*

*And yet.*

She closed the book, blew out the candle, and lay a long while in the dark, listening to the castle settle around her, before sleep — and, though she did not know it yet, the mist, and the woman who no longer walked ahead of her but had, at last, begun to turn around — came for her again.

Before the story moves on, it is worth knowing more fully who these two women were, and the paths — one noble, one rural; one gilded, one humble — that brought them both to the gardens of Castle Albrecht.

## Part One: Roots

### I. Rowan's Lineage — The Line of Women

Rowan was born into a valley where life followed the rhythms of sowing and harvest, yet her family always stood slightly apart. For generations, its women had been healers, midwives, and keepers of old knowledge. They knew which root cooled a fever, which flower brought restful sleep, and which herb coaxed strength back into a mother after childbirth.

But their legacy was not only one of plants and charms. In Rowan's family, love between women had long been accepted as part of their way. While the wider village turned its head or kept silent, within the family it was honored as something natural and enduring. Many of Rowan's foremothers had chosen female companions — partners who shared in their work, their hearths, and their secrets. To them, such bonds were seen as a gift: a love that mirrored the closeness of soil and seed, healer and patient, earth and rain.

Her grandmother often told her: *"Our power lives in two places, child: in the garden, and in the arms of the women we trust."*

Outsiders respected the family's skills but never fully embraced them. Whispers clung to their name — witches, charmers, women who kept to themselves. To Rowan, though, their world was one of warmth, where stories, remedies, and loves were handed down like heirlooms. She grew up with the understanding that she belonged to a lineage of women bound by healing, secrecy, and love for one another.

When disaster struck — whether through war, plague, or storm — Rowan's family home was shattered. Many of the women who had been her mentors, her kin, and her examples of quiet love were lost. She carried with her only fragments: a pouch of seeds, a bundle of dried herbs, and the memory of their wisdom.

Arriving at the castle as a wanderer, Rowan found refuge under the wing of the aging healer, who quickly recognized her heritage. She apprenticed there, and when the old woman died, Rowan took over the gardens and the stillroom.

Now she is known in the castle as a healer and gardener, her gentle presence calming the sick and troubled. Yet behind her soft-spoken demeanor lies the strength of her family's tradition: an inheritance of women's love and care that lives on in her hands. It gives her a rare sensitivity to the duchess's secret longings, and perhaps explains why Rowan's garden feels like more than a patch of soil — it is a sanctuary of desire, morality, and hidden truths, where the love of women has always found a way to bloom.

### II. House Veyrac's Daughter — Eleonore

Eleonore was born into House Veyrac, a noble line whose glory had long since begun to fade. Though their coffers thinned, the Veyracs clung to their reputation for refinement and learning. Eleonore's parents, determined to preserve what dignity remained, shaped her into the perfect ornament of their house: graceful, educated, and unfailingly polite.

But Eleonore absorbed more than the polished manners expected of her. From the family's aging apothecary she learned botany and the hidden virtues of plants; from her mother's neglected library she discovered forbidden volumes on astrology, alchemy, and the old mystical arts. She devoured these texts in secret, long after curfew, her candle burning low.

**The Rebel Beneath the Lace**

To the world, Eleonore was slender, blond, and soft-spoken — a gentle creature with a voice like a lullaby. But beneath that exterior lay a mind hungry for knowledge and a spirit that chafed at limits. She embroidered stars when she was supposed to stitch roses, pressed herbs into secret books instead of prayer journals, and slipped questions to the apothecary about the nature of life and death that no duchess-in-waiting should ever ask.

Her parents often scolded her: *"A lady's curiosity should never outpace her duty."* Yet Eleonore kept her secrets, hiding fragments of rebellion in every quiet moment.

**Marriage as a Bargain**

The marriage to the duke was a calculation — an alliance to secure her family's dwindling fortunes. Eleonore accepted, outwardly dutiful, but inwardly aware that she was being bartered. She met the duke at a ball arranged by her parents, where her beauty and kindness won his attention. Behind her smile, however, Eleonore told herself: *"If I must play this role, then I will shape it to my own design."*

Life in the castle gilded her cage. She was admired, envied, and gossiped about in equal measure. Some courtiers whispered she was too perfect, too serene — surely ambition stirred beneath that calm face. They were not wrong. Eleonore's rebellion was subtle: in her choice of books, in her secret notes hidden in herbals, in the way she turned the duke's expectations into freedoms where she could.

**Eleonore's Hidden Self**

Her passion for plants and hidden lore became her solace. The gardens were her true kingdom, where she could walk without performance, touching each leaf as if it held a secret. Among herbs and flowers she felt closest to herself, and it was here she longed for companionship — not the cold bond of alliance, but a connection of spirit, mind, and heart.

Her gentle voice and kind nature were not a mask but a weapon: a way to disarm suspicion, to make space for her quieter rebellions. Yet those who truly looked into her eyes might see it — the spark of someone who yearned for more than the role she had been assigned.

This makes Eleonore both beloved and slightly dangerous — admired for her kindness, but also unsettling to those who sense she isn't entirely what she appears.

### III. Duke Edward of Albrecht

Edward of Albrecht was born into a long-standing noble house renowned for discipline and loyalty to the crown. His life was shaped by duty from the beginning: he was trained in the arts of war, taught to manage estates, and raised to believe that a nobleman's honor rests on his word, his alliances, and his responsibilities to his people.

Edward grew into a man respected more for his steadiness than for brilliance. On the battlefield he was reliable, a commander who inspired trust by never breaking formation. At court he was admired for his integrity, though few would call him charming.

**His First Marriage & Henry**

Edward married young, choosing Lady Margaret of Stenford, whose family brought strength and influence. Their marriage was companionable if not warm, and produced a son, Henry. Margaret died when Henry was still a boy, leaving Edward both widower and sole parent.

Henry grew into a spirited young man — intelligent, restless, and far more sociable than his father. Edward, for all his discipline, found himself at odds with his son's free-spirited nature. He loves Henry and wishes to guide him, but his sternness often comes across as distance. Their relationship is marked by a mix of pride and quiet strain.

**His Marriage to Eleonore**

By the time Edward married Eleonore of Veyrac, he was already a man seasoned by responsibility and loss. The marriage was arranged for practical reasons: her family's noble name and his need for an alliance. Edward respected her beauty, admired her poise, and treated her with courtesy and fairness.

But though honorable, Edward was not a man of passion. His heart belonged to duty and legacy more than to intimacy. To him, Eleonore was a gracious duchess and a capable mistress of his household — but he never saw the restless spark in her, nor sensed the hidden longings that stirred behind her gentle smile.

**Edward's Character**

Edward is not cruel, nor is he blind to Eleonore's kindness. He values her and ensures she is well cared for. His honor makes him a respected lord among his peers, and his fairness earns the loyalty of his servants. Yet he is bound by tradition, unable to imagine that Eleonore could need something beyond her role as duchess.

He represents the safe, stable world — one that Eleonore honors but quietly grows beyond. Because Edward is decent and upright, Eleonore's feelings for Rowan cannot be explained away as rebellion against cruelty. Instead, Rowan awakens something deeper: recognition, companionship, and the flowering of a love that is tender, patient, and true. Rowan does not replace what Edward fails to give; she offers what only she can — a bond of spirit and heart that transcends duty.

In these last years, too, the King's demands upon him had grown heavier. Envoys came more often, and left less satisfied; the maps in his study grew more marked with red ink than any duke of Albrecht had needed in a generation. Edward, ever dutiful, knew that sooner or later his sword and his counsel would be called north, whatever the cost to the quiet order he had spent his life building — and some part of him, though he never said so aloud, had already begun to make his peace with it.

### IV. Henry, Son of the House

Henry of Albrecht was born to Edward's first wife and grew to be everything his father was not: quick-tongued, curious about the world beyond the castle walls, and possessed of a restless heart that chafed against the rigid expectations of his house. Where Edward measured his worth in duty fulfilled, Henry measured his in questions asked. It made him a difficult son to raise, and later, a difficult heir to predict.

He was not much older than Eleonore herself — a fact the household noted quietly and never aloud, in the way households notice everything and say nothing — and when his father brought home a bride nearer Henry's own age than his own, Henry had told himself, with the easy confidence of a young man certain of his own good sense, that it changed nothing between them.

It was not entirely true, and had not been true for some years. He had watched Eleonore grow from a shy, watchful bride into the quiet, steady heart of his father's household, had watched her kindness settle over the castle like weather, and had told no one — least of all himself, in any words he was willing to keep — how much of his own regard for her had stopped, long ago, being merely filial. He was, above all, a man loyal to his father, and loyalty had kept the feeling folded small and unspoken in some drawer of his heart he did not open, even for himself.

He believed, in the quiet certainty of a man who has never been tested, that he could carry it that way forever: unopened, unspoken, harmless.

## Part Two: The Turning of the Seasons — Year One

It was some days after that first distant glimpse from the terrace that Eleonore found an excuse to walk in the gardens herself, telling herself it was only for the air.

### Spring — First Awakening

The castle gardens after a morning rain. The clouds are breaking, shafts of pale sunlight falling on beds of green. The air is heavy with the scent of wet earth, rosemary, and lavender. Rowan is kneeling, her apron damp, hands stained with soil. She hums softly under her breath, a tune from her childhood. Eleonore appears at the archway, unseen at first. She pauses, watching.

**Eleonore** *(softly, to herself)*: "How peaceful it looks here… as though the world beyond these walls does not exist."

*Rowan looks up at the sound of her voice, her expression calm but guarded. A few strands of hair have fallen loose against her cheek. She wipes her hands on her apron as Eleonore steps closer.*

**Rowan**: "My lady. The paths are still wet — you'll soil your hem."

**Eleonore** *(smiling faintly)*: "Let them. It is worth the risk, if it brings me here."

*Rowan arches a brow but says nothing, returning to the herb bed. Eleonore kneels beside her without hesitation, gathering her skirts. The damp earth seeps cold through the fine fabric, but she does not flinch. Instead, she dips her fingers into the soil, curious.*

**Eleonore**: "It feels alive. As though it breathes beneath my touch."

**Rowan** *(glancing at her, voice quieter)*: "You notice what most do not."

*There is admiration in her tone, though carefully hidden. Eleonore looks up quickly, catching Rowan's gaze. The silence between them is thick with unspoken things. Rowan shifts, reaching for a sprig of thyme, breaking it gently.*

**Rowan**: "Here. Rub the leaves between your fingers."

*Eleonore does so, and the sharp green scent bursts into the air. She inhales, eyes closing briefly in delight. Rowan watches her more than the plant.*

**Eleonore** *(soft laugh)*: "How miraculous, that such a small thing can hold so much fragrance. It feels almost like… a secret."

**Rowan** *(studying her intently)*: "Perhaps it is."

*Eleonore's hand lingers near Rowan's, their fingers nearly touching again as they hold the thyme. She does not pull away. Instead, her voice drops lower, almost confessional.*

**Eleonore**: "Do you ever feel, Rowan, that you are meant for secrets? That you walk through the world carrying something no one else can see?"

*Rowan's breath catches. For a heartbeat, she considers the truth of her lineage, her whispered traditions, the quiet magic she carries. But she only offers a careful smile.*

**Rowan**: "Some things cannot be spoken in halls of stone. The earth keeps them safe until the right soul listens."

*Their eyes meet again — and this time, neither looks away at once. The garden hums with life around them: bees in the rosemary, the call of a dove, the drip of water from the eaves.*

**Eleonore** *(barely above a whisper)*: "Then perhaps… I wish to listen."

*For a long moment, Rowan's roughened hand remains beneath Eleonore's, soil-stained yet warm. The air stills around them, as if even the garden holds its breath. But the truth pressed between them — unspoken, trembling — was too large for that morning. Rowan's fingers tightened once, a fleeting promise, before she let go, turning back to the soil, movements careful, deliberate, as if grounding herself in the work. Eleonore watches her in silence, heart unsteady, her own hand resting lightly in her lap. She does not wipe the soil away. Instead, she cherishes the trace of contact — fragile proof that something has begun.*

**Eleonore's Reflection.** Later that afternoon, Eleonore withdraws to her chamber. The rain has begun again, pattering softly against the tall windows. She sets aside her embroidery untouched, her hands still faintly stained from the soil. A trace of thyme lingers on her skin, sharp and green. She brings her fingers to her lips, as if to catch the memory.

*"I wished to listen… and she heard me,"* she whispers.

She rises and walks to the mirror. There is a smudge of earth at the edge of her sleeve, dark against silk. Normally she would summon a maid to tidy her appearance, but now she leaves it as it is — proof that she was there, with Rowan, her hands in the soil like any common woman.

*How strange,* she thinks, *that the memory of a touch can weigh more heavily than a room full of courtly compliments. How strange, that I should crave not flattery nor jewels, but the warmth of a gardener's hand in mine.*

She clasps her hands together, pressing them against her heart. The garden soil is gone now, but in her chest the seed has already taken root. She knows it — though she cannot yet name what it will grow to become.

### Summer — Blossoming Connection

A blazing midsummer afternoon. The gardens are humming with bees, the air thick with the scent of roses and sage. Rowan works beneath a shaded arbor, cutting herbs and tying them into bundles. Eleonore enters with a parasol, though she soon closes it, preferring the dappled sunlight that filters through the vines.

**Eleonore** *(playful)*: "Every time I come, I find you bent over another basket of leaves. Do you never tire of green?"

*Rowan looks up, sweat glistening faintly at her temple, but she does not pause her hands.*

**Rowan** *(dryly, though with the ghost of a smile)*: "Green does not tire of me, my lady. It clings to me, whether I wish it or not."

*Eleonore laughs, a soft musical sound that makes Rowan glance at her again, longer this time. Encouraged, Eleonore steps closer, trailing her fingers across a rosemary bush. She inhales deeply.*

**Eleonore**: "This place feels different when you are in it. Alive. Almost enchanted."

**Rowan** *(shrugging, though her eyes flicker with something deeper)*: "Enchantment is only patience and listening. Plants grow the way hearts beat — whether watched or not."

**Eleonore** *(lowering her voice, teasing)*: "Then tell me… what does my heart reveal, when you listen?"

*The question hangs between them, bold as sunlight. Rowan stills, the herb bundle half-tied in her hands.*

**Rowan** *(quiet, guarded)*: "More than I should dare to say."

*Their eyes lock. A servant's laughter carries faintly from the distant orchard, breaking the spell. Rowan looks back to her work, fingers tightening the knot.*

**Eleonore** *(after a pause, gently)*: "You speak as though words are dangerous."

**Rowan** *(low)*: "Sometimes they are."

*Silence falls, but it is not empty. Eleonore slowly sits on the bench beside Rowan's basket, folding her hands neatly in her lap. For once, she does not fill the air with chatter — she only sits near, breathing in the scents of crushed herbs and summer heat.*

**Eleonore** *(soft, after a long silence)*: "When I was a child, I dreamed of secrets. Of hidden worlds behind locked doors. Now I think perhaps the hidden things were never far away at all… only waiting for me to open my eyes."

*Rowan looks at her, struck by the honesty in her tone. For a heartbeat, she almost reaches for Eleonore's hand — but instead she presses another sprig of rosemary into her palm.*

**Rowan**: "Then keep this. A reminder. Sometimes the smallest things carry the deepest truths."

*Eleonore closes her hand around it, her fingers brushing Rowan's as she takes it. The warmth between them is heavy as the summer air — not spoken, not declared, but undeniable.*

**Eleonore's Reflection.** The afternoon heat lingers even within the cool stone halls of the castle. Eleonore returns to her chambers with a faint flush upon her cheeks, the sprig of rosemary carefully hidden within her folded hands.

*"Words too dangerous…"* she whispers to herself, *"and yet… what danger could I wish more than silence?"*

She moves to her writing desk and takes out a small book — her private notebook of pressed flowers and herbal notes — and tucks the rosemary sprig between its pages. For a moment she hovers, wondering if she dares to write the name — Rowan — in her own hand. She does not. Not yet.

*"If words are dangerous,"* she says softly, *"then let silence keep me safe… until my heart can no longer bear it."*

Outside her window, the summer air hums with cicadas. In her chest, something hums too — a secret rhythm, wild and new.

### Autumn — Deepening Intimacy

Late afternoon in early autumn. The castle garden glows with amber light. Leaves drift down from the orchard trees, and the last blooms of the season nod heavy with seed. Rowan kneels near a low stone wall, gathering dried herbs into baskets for winter storage. Eleonore enters, wearing a cloak lined with soft fur, her hair braided against the breeze.

**Eleonore** *(smiling softly)*: "Summer has left you with a great harvest. It's as if you've trapped the season in your baskets."

*Rowan looks up, her fingers dusty with crushed lavender.*

**Rowan** *(quiet)*: "Only trying to keep what might be needed later. Winter spares nothing."

**Eleonore** *(kneeling slightly, reaching to touch a dried blossom)*: "And yet you coax life from the earth even as it fades. You make endings beautiful."

*Rowan glances at her, startled by the intimacy of the words. Eleonore's cloak brushes the soil as she crouches beside her. Rowan's fingers still, trembling faintly as she ties off the bundle of herbs.*

**Rowan**: "You speak as though I had power over such things. I only tend what's given."

**Eleonore** *(whispering)*: "And do you not see what you've given me?"

*Silence. The words hang between them like a leaf caught mid-fall. Rowan swallows hard, looking down at her hands. Her voice, when it comes, is low and rough.*

**Rowan**: "My lady… you mustn't."

**Eleonore** *(gently)*: "I must. Or else my heart will wither before the frost."

*A gust of wind scatters leaves around them. One lands on Rowan's shoulder; Eleonore reaches out to brush it away. Her fingers graze the curve of Rowan's neck — not an accident this time, but a soft, deliberate stroke. Rowan closes her eyes for a heartbeat, breathing in sharply, before taking Eleonore's hand and holding it — not pulling away, not yet.*

**Rowan** *(whispering)*: "You don't know what you risk."

**Eleonore** *(firmly, though her voice shakes)*: "Perhaps I do. And perhaps I would risk more."

*Rowan opens her eyes. In them, Eleonore sees both fear and yearning — an entire life of restraint trembling at the edge of surrender. But Rowan lets her hand remain, clasped in Eleonore's. A bell tolls from the castle tower, distant and mournful. They do not move.*

*The garden in autumn is a place of endings, but also of seeds hidden in the earth, waiting for spring. In the clasp of their hands, something fragile and fierce begins to root.*

**Eleonore's Reflection.** The light in her chamber is dimmer now; the sun sets earlier, the shadows stretch longer. Eleonore closes the door softly behind her and leans against it, her cloak still on, her pulse still running from the garden.

*"Rowan…"* she whispers, taking the name as if it were a prayer.

*It was not like the stolen glances of court,* she thinks, *not like the murmured gallantries of nobles. It was steady. Frightened. Fierce. The kind of touch that does not seduce but anchors. The kind that tells you that you are seen.*

She opens the small book of pressed herbs and adds a dried lavender sprig alongside the rosemary from the summer before.

*"Two seasons,"* she murmurs. *"Two tokens."*

*I thought I understood longing,* she thinks. *I did not. Not until her.*

### The Steward's Eye

Godfrey had served House Albrecht for thirty years, first as the old duke's page and now as steward of the household, and in all that time he had prided himself on missing nothing. It was Godfrey who noticed that the duchess's evening walks had grown longer, that she returned from the gardens with color in her cheeks that no exertion with a trowel could account for, and that the gardener Rowan — a woman of no family, no dowry, no claim to anyone's attention — was spoken of in the kitchens with an odd, protective fondness, as though the servants shared a secret they were keeping from him.

He said nothing for a long while, turning it over as he turned over the household accounts, patiently, suspiciously. Then, one grey afternoon, he found an excuse to corner Rowan in the stillroom, asking pointed questions about which hours she kept and for whom.

**Godfrey** *(mild, watching her closely)*: "The duchess visits you often, I'm told. Later than a lady ought to be out among the beds, some evenings."

**Rowan** *(steady, not looking up from her mortar)*: "Her Grace has an interest in remedies. I answer what she asks."

**Godfrey**: "Mm. And does Her Grace often ask her questions after the household is abed?"

*Before Rowan can answer, the door opens and Henry steps in, unhurried, as though he had been looking for exactly this room.*

**Henry** *(easily)*: "Godfrey. Good — you can settle an argument. I've told my father his physician bleeds him too often for his headaches, and that Rowan's willow-bark tonic does more good than a lancet. I've been coming to her myself these past weeks. Ask her yourself what hour I generally trouble her — you'll find it's later than is polite, and entirely my own fault."

*Godfrey looks between them, his suspicion visibly wrong-footed by so plain and plausible an explanation from the heir himself.*

**Godfrey** *(after a pause, stiffly)*: "I meant no accusation, my lord. Only the household's good order."

**Henry** *(clapping him lightly on the shoulder, already steering him toward the door)*: "Of course. No one keeps better order than you, Godfrey. Walk with me — I want your opinion on the east wall repairs."

*He glances back once at Rowan as he goes, the smallest nod passing between them: safe, for now. When the door closes behind them, Rowan sets down her mortar and finds that her hands are not quite steady.*

*He told himself, walking Godfrey firmly toward the east wall, that he had done it only for the household's peace. He did not examine too closely why the thought of the duchess named in gossip had put such an edge in his voice, nor why some part of him had wanted, quite badly, to be the one who kept her safe.*

### Winter — The Turning Point

A snow-laden evening. The garden is silent under a soft white blanket, its paths unmarked except for two sets of footprints leading to the glasshouse at its edge. Inside, the air is warmer, scented with damp earth and the faint perfume of overwintering herbs. Frost clings to the panes, blurring the world beyond.

*Rowan stands inside the glasshouse, lighting a small oil lamp. The glow softens the shadows of her face. The door creaks; Eleonore slips in, wrapped in a heavy cloak, her breath misting in the chill. She closes the door quietly behind her.*

**Eleonore** *(hushed)*: "I thought I might find you here."

**Rowan** *(turning, startled but softening)*: "You should be by the fire, my lady. It's bitter tonight."

**Eleonore** *(smiling faintly)*: "Perhaps. But there's warmth enough here."

*Rowan looks at her — at the snow melting in her hair, at the pale glow of her face in the lamplight. Eleonore moves closer, the hush of the glasshouse making every sound more intimate.*

**Rowan** *(quietly)*: "You risk much, coming here after dark."

**Eleonore** *(firmly, though her voice trembles)*: "I am tired of risking only in my thoughts."

*Rowan exhales, her breath shuddering. She sets the lamp on a low table among the potted herbs. For a long moment they simply look at each other — the silence between them full of everything they have not said.*

**Rowan** *(low, almost breaking)*: "I tried to keep this safe. I tried to keep you safe."

**Eleonore** *(stepping closer)*: "And yet here we are."

*She lifts her hands and slowly lowers her hood. Her hair spills free, pale and soft against the dark of her cloak. Rowan's eyes close briefly, as if the sight is too much. When she opens them, Eleonore is standing only a breath away.*

**Eleonore** *(whispering)*: "Do you think me so fragile? That my heart cannot bear the truth?"

*Rowan trembles; her hands hover between them, unsure whether to reach or retreat. Eleonore takes them gently, turning the calloused palms upward, pressing them to her own heart through the heavy cloak.*

**Eleonore** *(soft, steady)*: "Here. Feel it. It beats for you."

*For a heartbeat, Rowan does not move. Then she closes her fingers over Eleonore's, holding on as if anchoring herself. The lamp flickers; a petal drifts from a wintering plant and lands on the table between them.*

**Rowan** *(broken whisper)*: "If this is a sin, I cannot repent it."

**Eleonore** *(leaning closer, her forehead against Rowan's)*: "Then let it be no sin. Let it be what it is."

*They stand there in the lamplight, foreheads touching, hands entwined, the scent of damp earth and winter herbs wrapping around them. No kiss yet — only the surety of two hearts meeting at last without disguise. Outside, snow begins to fall harder, sealing the world beyond in silence.*

*Winter has stripped the world bare. Yet in the glasshouse, two women hold a warmth that cannot be buried, no matter how deep the frost.*

**Eleonore's Reflection.** The corridors of the castle are silent, muffled by the snow. Eleonore's boots leave faint wet marks on the stone as she climbs the stairs. Her cloak is still heavy with cold, but beneath it, her skin is warm where Rowan's hands had pressed.

*"Her hands…"* she whispers. *"Her hands were trembling."*

*I had thought myself gentle,* she thinks. *I had thought myself obedient. But there in the glasshouse, I was not a duchess, not a wife, not a daughter of any family. I was simply a woman whose heart had found its answer.*

She opens her notebook and adds a third token: a tiny white petal fallen from the wintering plant in the glasshouse.

*"Three seasons,"* she says softly. *"Three tokens. How can I hide what has already rooted in me?"*

*If winter strips everything bare,* she thinks, *then let it strip me too. I will stand as I am — unadorned, unmasked — and wait for the spring she has promised me with her eyes.*

### Spring Returns — Blossoming into Love

A soft, misty morning. The garden, once bare, is now pale green with new shoots and buds. The glasshouse stands open, windows propped to let in the fresh air. Rowan kneels by a bed of early flowers, planting seedlings with careful hands. Eleonore walks toward her without her cloak, her gown simple, her hair loose around her shoulders. There is no one else about — the castle is still at breakfast.

*Rowan looks up as Eleonore approaches, brushing soil from her palms. Her eyes widen slightly at the sight of the duchess unadorned, sunlight catching her hair. Eleonore kneels beside her without hesitation, the damp earth soaking the hem of her dress.*

**Eleonore** *(smiling softly)*: "Another spring. And yet everything feels… new."

**Rowan** *(quiet, searching her face)*: "Some things survive the winter stronger than before."

*Eleonore reaches for a seedling and helps press it into the soil. Their hands meet over the little plant, fingers curling into each other's. This time neither of them pulls away.*

**Eleonore** *(low, certain)*: "I have no fear left, Rowan."

*Rowan trembles, her lips parting as though to speak but no words come. Eleonore shifts closer, their knees touching in the damp soil.*

**Rowan** *(whisper)*: "My lady…"

**Eleonore** *(firm, tender)*: "Not here. Not now. Not 'my lady.'"

*Rowan's breath catches. Slowly, she lifts a hand to Eleonore's face, brushing a stray wisp of hair from her cheek. Eleonore leans into the touch.*

**Rowan** *(softly, almost disbelief)*: "Eleonore…"

*The sound of her name in Rowan's mouth makes Eleonore close her eyes. She leans forward, their foreheads touching as before — but now she tilts her face, and at last their lips meet, a hesitant, trembling kiss that deepens as their hands clutch at each other in the soil.*

*The garden is no longer a place of secrecy. It is a sanctuary, a beginning. The seed planted in spring, hidden through summer, ripened in autumn, and sheltered through winter now flowers between them — bright, fearless, alive.*

*They draw back slightly, breathless but smiling. Eleonore's fingers stay entwined with Rowan's, soil smudging their skin. Neither of them cares.*

**Eleonore** *(whisper, smiling through tears)*: "This is what I prayed for in silence."

**Rowan** *(smiling back, eyes shining)*: "And this is what I kept safe for you."

*The morning mist lifts; the sun spills gold over the new growth. In its light, two women kneel side by side, no longer duchess and servant but lovers, planting the first flowers of a life they will build together.*

**Eleonore's Reflection.** I feel as though I have stepped out of myself and into a dream. The garden is no longer just earth and roots, no longer just the green work of hands. It is a sanctuary of truth — of my truth. Rowan's lips still burn against mine; the taste of earth and rain and something sweeter lingers.

How could something so simple — soil beneath us, seedlings trembling in their beds — hold so much power? I was afraid once. Afraid of whispers, of duty, of the weight of titles pressing down like a stone. But in that moment, kneeling in the dirt with Rowan's hand warm in mine, I felt no chains at all.

Her name on my tongue… *Rowan*. I spoke it without fear, and she answered with my own as if it belonged to her. There is no courtly mask in her, no hollow flattery. She sees me — not as a duchess, not as a wife bound by alliance, but as Eleonore. As a woman whose heart still dares to bloom.

I do not know what storms will come. The castle holds sharp eyes and sharper tongues, and the world beyond these walls is colder still. Yet for the first time in so long, I feel strong enough to face it. For I am not alone.

If love is sin, then let me be a sinner. If love is exile, then let me walk with her far from every stone and banner. For what is nobility, what is duty, compared to the grace of her touch?

The earth keeps secrets — seeds in darkness, roots hidden deep. Now I, too, will keep this secret: not out of fear, but out of reverence. Until the day comes when we need no shadows, when Rowan and I may walk beneath the sun together, unashamed.

Spring has returned, and with it, so have I.

**Rowan's Reflection.** The soil is still under my nails. Her warmth is still in my palms.

I have touched Eleonore's hand a hundred times before — guiding her fingers to a stem, a leaf, a root. But today was different. Today her hand stayed. Today her lips found mine.

I have lived a life of waiting. Waiting for seasons to turn, for seedlings to sprout, for herbs to root, for danger to pass. And waiting, always waiting, for the moment when I might stop hiding the truest parts of myself. It has been a long winter in my soul. Today, at last, I felt spring break open inside me.

She is so unlike me: light where I am shadow, grace where I am steadiness. Yet in her I find a mirror — not of my face but of my longing. She looks at me as though I am more than a servant, more than a healer, more than a remnant of my family's old magic. She looks at me as though I belong.

And I am afraid. Afraid of what will come when the castle awakens to this quiet rebellion planted in its garden. Afraid of the Duke's household, of gossip, of ruin. But also — and this is the deeper truth — I am no longer afraid enough to stop.

When she whispered my name it was as though the earth itself called me home. When she kissed me, it was not the soft, timid thing I had imagined in my sleepless nights; it was steady, certain, like the push of a seed through soil.

I have spent my life coaxing green things to grow in silence. Now something green and wild grows in me, and I will tend it with all the care I have ever given a garden. Even if we must remain hidden for now, even if this love is fragile, I will keep it safe.

Spring has returned to the garden. But this time, it has returned to me as well.

### A Secret Night in the Garden

The castle slept. Its stone corridors echoed only with the wind, while the garden beyond lay hushed in silver moonlight. The glasshouse glowed faintly, its panes catching the stars.

Eleonore slipped from the shadow of the great hall, her cloak held tight. Her heart thudded as she crossed the damp grass, for she knew whom she would find waiting. And there she was — Rowan, standing among the herbs, her dark hair gleaming like wet silk, her green eyes catching every trace of starlight.

Neither spoke at first. Eleonore reached out with trembling fingers, brushing Rowan's cheek. Rowan caught her hand and pressed it to her lips, closing her eyes as if the gesture itself were prayer.

*"Eleonore,"* Rowan whispered, the name breaking like water.

The Duchess could not answer in words. She pulled Rowan close, their mouths meeting in a kiss that was no longer hesitant or searching, but deep, certain. Rowan's hands moved to her waist, while Eleonore's fingers slid into the dark braid, loosening it until strands fell across Rowan's shoulders.

The air between them warmed with the rush of breath and the brush of cloth. Eleonore's cloak slipped to the ground unnoticed. They stumbled together against the wooden bench, laughter breaking between kisses, soft and breathless. Rowan murmured against her ear, and Eleonore shivered, answering with a sigh that left no doubt what she wanted.

Their touches grew slower then, more deliberate, the air thick with a tension that was no longer innocent but irresistible. Foreheads touched, hands lingered. Every movement spoke what neither dared to name aloud.

And then — the garden kept its secrets.

The night folded around them, the moon drawing a veil of silver over glass and leaves. What passed between them beyond that veil belonged to them alone: a union of trust, of hunger, of love that had waited through four seasons and now finally bloomed.

## Part Three: The Year of Trial — Year Two

### The King's Summons

The messenger came in early autumn of the second year, his horse blown, his King's colors dark with road-dust. He was shown to Edward's study within the hour, and by evening the whole castle knew, in the particular way castles always know things before they are told: the hill lords beyond the Greywater had broken the old truce, burned two border villages, and put the King's garrison at Thornhold to the sword. Edward was summoned north — not merely to send men, as he had in past seasons, but to go himself, sword and seal both, and treat with what remained of the King's authority in that country.

He told Eleonore himself, that evening, in the small dining hall where they had shared so many quiet, dutiful meals.

**Edward**: "I leave within the week. Henry rides with me as far as the river; the King wishes him seen, so the border lords know the succession is settled and secure. You will govern the household in my absence, as you have done before."

**Eleonore** *(quietly)*: "How long?"

**Edward**: "As long as it takes to make men behave who have decided not to." *(A pause; something almost gentle crosses his face.)* "You need not fear for the estate. You have run it as well as I have these many winters, whether either of us has said so aloud."

**Eleonore** *(surprised into honesty)*: "You have never said so."

**Edward** *(a rare, rueful almost-smile)*: "No. I find I am saying a great many things this week I have left too long unsaid. It is what leaving does to a man, I think — makes him suddenly generous with a currency he was always too proud to spend."

*It was, in eleven years of marriage, the nearest Edward ever came to telling her he loved her, and though it fell short of the word itself, Eleonore would remember it, later, as a kindness she had not expected and did not deserve to have expected so little.*

He left three days later at the head of a modest column, Henry riding beside him in his father's colors, and Eleonore watched from the gatehouse until the road took them from sight, feeling, against all her own better judgment, a genuine fear for a man she had never managed to love and had somehow, over eleven quiet years, come to need.

### News from the Border

Three weeks brought no word, which was ordinary; the fourth week brought a letter in Henry's hand, brief and badly controlled, saying only that his father lived and that they were bringing him home. The fifth week brought Edward himself, carried on a litter through the same gatehouse he had ridden out of a season's turning gone, his face grey and slack, a wound above his temple hidden under linen gone brown at the edges.

The physicians said what physicians say when they do not know: that the head is a strange country, that some men wake in a day and some in a season and some not at all, that time, and rest, and God's mercy would tell what neither medicine nor prayer could promise.

It was Rowan, in the end, who was sent for — not by Eleonore, who could not bring herself to ask it of her, but by the household physician himself, out of simple professional respect for the healer whose remedies had quietly outperformed his own for years. Rowan came to the duke's bedside and did what she could: cooling compresses, the bitter tinctures her grandmother had taught her for fevers of the blood and the brain, a patience that outlasted the physicians' own.

She never once, in all the long weeks that followed, said aloud what it cost her to tend, with steady and skillful hands, the recovery of the one man whose death, however she would never have wished it, would have made her own life simpler. Eleonore, watching her from the doorway one evening, understood the gift being given her without a word passing between them, and loved Rowan, in that moment, more than she had in a year of gentler ones.

Edward did not wake. He breathed, and his color slowly returned, and his heart beat on with the same stubborn steadiness he had brought to every other duty of his life — but his eyes did not open, and the castle settled, uneasily, into the long grey work of waiting.

### What Henry Saw

Henry took up the burden of the household the way he took up everything: too fast, and with more feeling than his father would ever have shown, snapping at stewards one day and weeping alone in the chapel the next, though he let no one see the second. He had come home from the border a harder man than he had left it, having watched his father cut down under him and carried him three days by litter through country still thick with the King's enemies, and grief and fear together had worn away whatever boyishness had once let him pretend his feelings for Eleonore were simple.

He saw her now as a regent sees a duchess: capable, composed, holding the estate together through a crisis that would have broken lesser women, and it was this — her strength, more than her beauty, which he had admired for years without letting himself call it admiration — that finally undid the drawer in his heart he had kept so carefully shut.

It was a raw evening in early winter when he came upon the glasshouse by chance, meaning only to walk off an hour he could not spend usefully any other way, and saw through the fogged glass two women in the lamplight, and one of them was laughing — laughing, fully and without performance, in a way he had never once seen from her at his father's table in eleven years — with her hand held inside the gardener's, their heads bent close.

He did not go in. He did not make a sound. He stood in the cold a long moment, understanding arriving in him slow and total, like water finding every crack in a wall at once, and then he walked back to the castle alone, and told no one, and did not sleep that night, nor for several after.

### The Chancellor's Counsel

Lord Chancellor Bertrand arrived from the capital before the first snow, sent by the King to see the border settlement through and, more delicately, to see the succession of Albrecht settled alongside it. He was an old man, dry as parchment, and entirely without malice, which made his counsel harder to resist than cruelty would have been.

**Bertrand** *(to Henry, in the map room, not unkindly)*: "Your father may wake tomorrow, or in ten years, or never, my lord, and the King's peace cannot wait on a physician's guess. A duchy with an uncertain succession is an invitation to every ambitious neighbor within three days' ride — including, I need not remind you, the very hill lords who put your father in that bed."

**Henry** *(carefully)*: "My inheritance is not in question. I am my father's only son."

**Bertrand**: "Your inheritance, no. The duchess's dower lands and the Veyrac alliance, however, pass with her — to whatever house she next marries into, should your father not recover and she choose, in time, to remarry elsewhere. The King would rest easier, and so, I think, would you, if that alliance remained where it has always served the realm best: within these walls." *(A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat.)* "She is a fine woman, my lord. And you are unmarried. I say only what every man from here to the capital is already thinking."

*Henry said nothing for a long while, looking down at the map of a border he had bled to hold, and did not tell the Chancellor what he had seen through a glasshouse window, nor why the suggestion, which should have pleased him beyond every hope he had ever folded away unspoken, now sat in his chest like a stone.*

The Chancellor found occasion, some days later, to raise the same delicate matter with Eleonore herself, framing it as a King's concern for the realm's stability rather than any wish of his own — though Eleonore, no stranger to the language of alliances dressed as affection, heard the proposal plainly enough beneath the courtesy, and gave him no answer beyond a duchess's practiced grace, and carried the weight of it to her chamber alone that night.

### Henry's Proposal

He came to her a week later, in the small dining hall where she had once eaten so many quiet breakfasts with his father, and stood a long while before he could make himself begin.

**Henry**: "I am not going to pretend the Chancellor's counsel is only the King's. I have thought of little else for a week, and I find I cannot make myself sorry for thinking it, whatever else is true." *(A breath.)* "Eleonore. If my father does not wake — and God knows I pray daily that he does — the realm will want this settled, and I find I do not only want it settled. I have wanted you, I think, longer than I've let myself say so, even to myself."

*Eleonore is very still, her hands folded in her lap, her face giving nothing away that he can read.*

**Eleonore** *(quietly)*: "Your father is not dead, Henry."

**Henry**: "No. And I would have him wake tomorrow and never think of this again, whatever that costs me. I am not asking you to wish him gone. I am asking you to know, when the day comes that this question can no longer be put off, that there is an answer already waiting that would cost you nothing — no scandal, no loss, only what the whole castle already half expects."

*He does not say, though it stands plainly in his face, that he already knows what it would truly cost her, and has come anyway, hoping against everything he saw through that fogged glass that hope might yet be permitted him.*

### Eleonore's Refusal — Quiet Defiance

**Eleonore** *(after a long silence, gently but without a single tremor)*: "You are a good man, Henry, and you deserve better than to have your heart handed back to you the way I must now hand it. But I will not marry you — not if your father wakes tomorrow, and not if he never wakes at all."

**Henry** *(quiet, already knowing, needing to hear it said)*: "Because of her."

*It is not truly a question, and Eleonore does not treat it as one. She meets his eyes instead, and answers with the same quiet steadiness that has carried her through every crisis this long year has asked of her.*

**Eleonore**: "Because of her. I did not choose it to wound anyone, least of all you, and I am sorry — more sorry than I can say — that you saw what you were never meant to carry alone. But I will not trade one bargain of alliance for another, Henry, not even a kinder one, not even yours. I have spent eleven years learning to live well within a marriage I did not choose. I find, now that I finally know what it is to be chosen freely instead, that I cannot go back to being merely useful."

*Henry looks at her a long moment, something in his face breaking and, slowly, mending itself into something steadier.*

**Henry** *(low)*: "I saw you both. In the glasshouse, in the winter before last. I told myself for a long time it meant less than it did."

**Eleonore** *(softly, unflinching)*: "And now?"

**Henry** *(a breath that is almost, not quite, a laugh)*: "Now I think I have spent a great deal of grief on a woman who was never going to be unhappy without me. There is a certain mercy in that, once the sting of it passes." *(He straightens, some old discipline of his father's surfacing in him at last.)* "I will tell the Chancellor the matter is closed. I will not tell him why, and I will not tell him what I saw, not now, not ever — you have my word on that, whatever else you think of me tonight."

**Eleonore** *(quietly, meaning it)*: "I think a great deal of you tonight, Henry. More than I did this morning."

*He bows to her, formal as a stranger and yet gentler than he has ever been with her, and leaves her alone in the small dining hall, where eleven years of quiet breakfasts have finally given way to something neither dutiful nor bargained — a kindness, freely given, from a man who had wanted, for a while, to be loved instead of merely spared.*

### Edward's Final Grace

Edward woke, in the end, on a grey morning in early spring — not fully, not for long, but enough. His eyes opened; his hand, when Eleonore took it, closed weakly around hers; and for perhaps an hour, before the fever that had lain coiled beneath his long sleep finally rose up to claim what the wound had not, he was entirely himself.

He said little. He asked after the harvest, and after Henry, and seemed satisfied, in his quiet way, with both answers. Then, holding her hand still, he looked at her with an attention she had not felt from him in all their years together, as though duty had finally worn thin enough to let something else show through.

**Edward** *(weak, but clear)*: "You have done well by this house, Eleonore. Better than I ever told you, which was my failing, not yours." *(A pause, gathering the last of his strength.)* "Whatever comes after me — let it be settled plainly. Henry inherits outright, my seal on it, no conditions laid on you. You have carried enough of other people's bargains. I'd not have my death be one more."

*Eleonore, unable to speak, presses his hand to her lips, and he seems, in the last clear moment his eyes are open, almost at peace with what he sees there — not the passion he never gave her and never asked for in return, but something he perhaps values more, at the very end: a debt, quietly and fully forgiven.*

He did not wake again. He died four days later, in his own bed, with Henry on one side of him and Eleonore on the other, and the household mourned him honestly, for whatever he had lacked in warmth he had never once lacked in fairness, and a castle remembers fairness longer than it remembers fire.

His dying word, entered into the official record by the Chancellor himself before he rode south again, settled every question of succession the border crisis had raised: Henry, Duke of Albrecht, unconditionally — and the dowager duchess Eleonore, free to order her own life as she saw fit, with the full honor and provision due her position, for as long as she lived.

No one asked her, after that, why she never remarried. It was assumed, quietly and universally, to be grief — and Eleonore, who had grieved Edward honestly, if not the way the household imagined, never troubled to correct them.

## Part Four: After

### The New Duke

Henry ruled well, in time — better, some said, than his father, for he governed with his father's fairness and a warmth Edward had never quite managed. He did not marry for some years, and when he did, it was a match his council was pleased with and his own heart had grown calm enough to welcome without complication. He was, by all accounts, a contented man, and if some old ember of what he had once felt for his stepmother never entirely went out, he tended it the way one tends the memory of a fever survived — with respect, and no wish at all to have it back.

He did not become Rowan and Eleonore's confidant, not in the easy way of old friends; there remained, always, a careful formality between them, the courtesy of a man who has made his peace with a wound rather than forgotten it. But he was fair, and he was grateful — to Eleonore for a mercy she had never framed as one, and to Rowan, in his own private accounting, for a healer's hands that had bought his father four more days of life than the border had any right to allow — and fairness and gratitude, tended patiently enough, grow eventually into something very near to respect.

It was several years into his reign, well after any grief had softened into ordinary memory, that he summoned Rowan to the hall and, with rather more ceremony than the moment strictly required, granted her the title of Keeper of the Gardens and the Healing House, with lodgings of her own choosing anywhere on the estate.

**Rowan** *(searching his face, understanding more than the writ said)*: "You did not have to make it formal, my lord."

**Henry** *(a small, hard-won smile)*: "No. But a household runs easier on what is written than on what is merely understood, and I find, these days, that I would rather my stepmother's happiness rest on something sturdier than my own good will. Wills change. Deeds do not, once sealed."

*It was not friendship, quite. But it was, in its own careful way, a kind of love — the kind a man learns to give once he has stopped needing to be loved back in the particular shape he once wanted.*

### Epilogue — A Garden in Full Bloom

Many years later, the dower house at the edge of the estate was known simply as the place where the dowager duchess and her companion kept their garden — the finest in the dukedom, visitors said, though none could quite say why two women's care should make it so.

Eleonore, her hair silvered now but her eyes as quick as ever, still walked the paths each morning before the household woke. Rowan, her hands still stained with soil though her back had grown less willing to kneel as easily as it once had, still hummed the old tune from her childhood over the herb beds. Their notebook of pressed flowers — rosemary, lavender, a single white petal, and dozens of tokens added in all the years since — sat on the shelf between their two chairs by the fire, worn soft at the corners from handling.

Duke Henry visited once or twice a season, always announced, always correct, and always, in his own reserved way, glad to come — bringing news of the realm, and once, memorably, his own small daughter, who took one look at the garden and refused to leave it until dark. No one in three generations of the household ever spoke the word *scandal* in connection with the dower house, nor needed to; a duke's quiet approval, once given, settles a matter more thoroughly than any denial ever could.

Eleonore never remarried, and the household, which had once wondered, stopped wondering long before she stopped being asked. She had, everyone agreed, given her heart entirely to her late husband's memory — and if the shape that devotion took each evening was a woman's hand in hers before the fire, well, grief was a private country, and no one thought it their place to draw its borders for her.

On quiet evenings, when the light went gold over the beds and the bees grew slow and heavy in the last of the summer roses, Eleonore would take Rowan's hand exactly as she had that first spring morning, soil and all, and Rowan would say, exactly as her grandmother once said to her, that their power lived in two places: in the garden, and in the arms of the women they trusted.

The line of women had not ended with Rowan. It had only found new soil, and taken root, and bloomed.

*— End —*
