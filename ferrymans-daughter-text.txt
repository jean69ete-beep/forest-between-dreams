###CHAPTER 1: The Shed

The river had gone down in the night, the way it did every October, and by morning it had left its bones showing.

Nora saw them from the workshop door with her first mug of tea warm against her wrist: the long grey spine of the landing mole, dry for the first time since March; the green-slicked stones beyond it; an iron mooring ring sunk in the mud that she had last seen at eleven, the day she dropped her father's good knife off the end of the mole and was not scolded. Up on the bank, well above any waterline, the ferry sat in her cradle with her seams tarred black for the winter, like a large animal that had decided to sleep through the cold and trusted the world not to mind.

It was a good morning for it. That was how Nora thought of it, without quite knowing what <i>it</i> was: a good morning for waiting.

The shed behind her had been the ferryman's long before it was hers. It still smelled of him under everything she had added: tar and rope and river damp, and over them now linseed oil and beeswax and the sweet dust of old wood opened for the first time in a century. Forty-one boards ran from the door to the workbench. She had counted them at six years old, standing very straight in her father's boots, and had never found a reason to stop knowing.

On the bench, laid across two trestles, lay the window.

It was a sash from a house two villages up, six panes in a frame that had been painted so many times the corners had gone round and soft. Nora had lifted the glass out that morning pane by pane, with the patience of a woman defusing something. One pane was cracked. The rest were only old, and the difference between those two words was, as far as she was concerned, the whole of her trade. You repaired what had failed. You did not repair what had merely aged. Most days she suspected it was the whole of her philosophy too.

She held one of the old panes up to the open door. The glass was a hundred and eighty years old, drawn and flattened by hand, and it was full of small waves. Through it the river moved. The mole bent. The far bank swam up and settled again, softened and a little crooked, the way water makes things, and it was not wrong. It was only the glass remembering how it had been made.

She was humming. It was low and wandering, a tune with no beginning and no particular end. She did not know it. She never did.

Above the shed a door opened and shut, and the slow steps began down the path: her father, come to see the water. He did this every morning. He had done it for fifty years, which was longer than she had been alive, and the river had never once failed to be there.

He stopped at the top of the mole with his hands behind his back and looked at it for a long time. Nora did not go out to him. At this hour they had never needed to speak.

"Two feet down since Tuesday," he said at last, to the river.

"I saw."

"Good."

That was the conversation. It was, she sometimes thought, as near to a love letter as the two of them had ever required.

He lingered a moment longer than the water could have asked, looking not at the river now but at the shed, at her in its doorway with a pane of old glass held up to the light. Then he turned and went back up the path. It was a look she had learned to leave alone, the way you leave a half-set joint alone: it would hold or it wouldn't, but it would not be hurried.

She went back to the bench. The putty on the first pane would take until noon to give, and there was nothing to do about that but let it. She picked up the cracked pane instead and held it to the light once more, and the river bent softly in it, and she hummed, and did not know it, and waited.

She was good at it. Waiting was, her father used to say, the one thing in the world that could not be done badly. It could only be done too early.

Ines arrived at noon in a car that announced itself from a quarter mile off, which was one of the things Nora liked best about her.

"Don't get up," Ines called down the path, though Nora had no intention of it. She came on with a covered dish held out in front of her like an offering, coat open, scarf trailing, a small quick woman who had never once entered a room without leaving it slightly rearranged. "It's lentils. Before you say anything, I know you have food. I've seen your food. Your food is a heel of bread and a wish."

"It's sourdough."

"It's a heel."

She set the dish on the one clear corner of the bench and kissed Nora on the cheek, smelling of cold air and cinnamon. Then she bent over the window with her hands clasped behind her back, the way people do in museums. "So this is the famous sash."

"From the ridge road. The glass is original."

"It looks like it's been through something."

"It's a hundred and eighty years old. Everything looks like that."

"Not me," said Ines, with dignity, and lowered herself onto the old crate by the door, which had been hers for years by right of use.

She told Nora about the dishwasher. It was a long story with a cast: her sister's husband, who loaded plates the way other men build ships in bottles, slowly, with great hope, and entirely in the wrong order; her sister, who had stopped speaking of it, which was how you knew it was serious; and Ines herself, who had made one remark at Sunday lunch and had been described ever since as "a lot." Nora laughed until she had to put down the putty knife. That, too, was one of the things Ines was for.

Then the story ran out, as Ines's stories did, in the middle of a breath, and she sat looking through the open door at the river.

"Is he still doing that?"

"What?"

"Coming down in the mornings."

"Every morning."

"He looks at you," Ines said.

"He looks at the water."

"He looks at the water for about ten seconds. Then he looks at the shed for about thirty. I've been counting. I learned from the best." She turned a mitten over in her hands. "He wants to say something to you."

"He's wanted to say something for thirty years." Nora picked the knife up again. "It hasn't spoiled yet."

Ines let that go, which was a kindness, and one she was capable of more often than she let on. She asked instead, lightly, the other question, the one she had stopped asking in the spring and taken up again in September. "And you? Anyone?"

"The window."

"Nora."

"It has a lovely molding."

"I'll ask again after the first frost," said Ines, rising and rewinding her scarf. "Don't give me that face. I ask the way you wait: regularly, and without hurry." At the door she turned. "Eat the lentils. Hot. With a spoon, like a person."

When the car had finally dwindled out of earshot, Nora lifted the lid. They were good lentils. They were, in fact, the only thing Ines could cook, and she had been bringing them down this path for fourteen years, and Nora had never once told her it was too much lentil. She ate standing in the doorway, looking at the river, and took the gift the way you take any gift when you know the whole cost of it: slowly, and without pretending it had cost nothing.

By evening the water had dropped another inch. Nora wrote it on the doorframe in pencil, at the bottom of the column of marks that ran down the wood beside the door, where the height of the river had been recorded for longer than anyone remembered. There were three hands in it: one she had never known, her father's, square and slanting, and, from the year she was six, her own. The marks went down the frame like the rungs of a ladder, each one a small stop on the way to the bottom of the year. Tomorrow's would be lower still. She was in no hurry to write it, and she would be there when it was time, which was, her father would have said, the right way round.

###CHAPTER 2: Counting Boards

There were sixty-three steps from the shed door to her father's back door, and Nora took them every evening at six with whatever there was to carry.

Tonight it was the rest of the lentils.

She had never counted to be safe, though people assumed so, on the rare occasions they noticed. She counted because a thing that had been counted could not surprise you. Forty-one boards across the workshop floor. Sixty-three steps up the path. Nine stairs to the loft over the shed where she slept, up a ladder-stair she had built herself the winter she came home. The world was a place she could cross, so long as she knew how far it was.

Her father's kitchen was low and warm and exactly as it had been when she was six, down to the fare board over the door, where the summer prices (walkers a dollar, bicycles two, dogs free) had been repainted every March for longer than she had been alive. The ferry ran from April to September now, for walkers and cyclists and anyone who wished the long way round to be the short way across. In winter it slept. In winter, her father said, the river was entitled to a rest from being useful.

He was at the table already, with the paper folded to a crossword he never finished. He looked up, and then at the dish in her hands.

"Lentils," he said.

"Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say they were hearty." He lifted the lid and breathed in with the gravity of a man assessing weather. "Tell Ines they're hearty."

"You tell her."

"I wouldn't know what to say," said her father, without any sense that this was remarkable.

Nora let it go by. She had been letting it go by for thirty-two years, and it had worn a groove in her, smooth and well-fitted, like the grooves in the oars on the wall.

They ate. The talk was of the things they always talked about, in the order they always talked about them. The water. The weather coming. The sash.

"Putty's taking?"

"Slowly."

"And the glass?"

"One cracked. The rest are old."

"Old's not a fault," said her father.

"That's the whole job."

He nodded as though a point of procedure had been settled. He reached for the bread, and Nora watched his hands, which she had always looked at first, in anyone: square, scarred across the knuckles by fifty years of rope and oar, unhurried even now when they shook a little at the edges. They were the hands of a man who had carried a great many people over a great deal of water and had never once spilled any of them. She did not know what they would do if they ever had to carry something he could not name.

"Your mother rang," he said.

"At four?"

"At four. She says Sunday."

"She always says Sunday."

"She says bring bread. The good loaf. Not the one that could hold a door open."

"That one is the best I've made."

"She said you'd say that." He kept his face quite still. "She said to tell you it's still a doorstop."

Her mother lived in town, laughed too loudly at funerals, and had not lived under this roof since Nora was fourteen, a state of affairs both her parents described, cheerfully and in identical words, as convenient. She rang on Wednesdays and Sundays and whenever she read something in the paper she thought Nora ought to know. She had been the first person Nora ever told. Across the Sunday table, at nineteen, with her hands flat on the cloth to keep them still, Nora had got as far as "There's something I should—" and her mother had said, before the sentence could finish itself, "Thank God. I thought you'd never tell us. The neighbors knew anyway." And then she had laughed, loud and long, and everyone at the table had laughed with her, and Nora had remembered it ever since as the day her mother laughed. It had not occurred to her, then or for years afterward, to remember it as anything else.

Her father had said nothing at all. The next morning he came down to the mole at his usual hour, and stood a little longer than the water needed, and when she came out of the shed with her tea, he held out his own mug to her. She took it. Neither of them said a word. She had thought about that morning for thirteen years, and it was still the best thing anyone had ever said to her.

Now he looked down at his plate. For a moment his mouth opened, as if there were something at the end of the sentence, and she kept her eyes on her bread and did not help him.

"There's rain coming Thursday," he said.

"I know."

"Good."

She washed and he dried. It had been that way since she was tall enough to reach the sink. At the door he held out the empty dish to her, and she took it, and for a moment they both had hold of it.

"Tell Ines," he said, "that they were good lentils."

"You tell her."

"I will," he said, and they both knew he wouldn't, and neither of them minded. It was an arrangement. It had worked for a very long time.

The night was clear and cold, and the river had gone so far down that the mole ran out into the dark further than she had ever seen it. She walked to the end, where the planks gave onto stones, and stood with her hands in her pockets, listening to a river that was now a long way off and talking quietly to itself.

She had rules. She would not have called them that. She would have called them habits, or the sort of thing a person who worked with her hands came to know about herself. She looked at people's hands first, because hands told you what a person would do before the person did. She did not begin what she could not finish. She did not make promises with the future folded into them. And since two winters ago, there was one more, which she kept on the shelf with the others and never took down to look at: <i>not as a first, and not as a lesson.</i>

It was a good rule. It had been paid for.

She was not unhappy. She would have liked that on the record, if anyone had been keeping one. She had her trade, her father, her friend with her lentils, a river that knew her name. The loneliness people imagined in a life like hers was mostly a failure of imagination on their part. Waiting was not the same as lacking. It was only what a person did while the river decided.

Only now and then, at the end of the mole in the dark, there came a feeling she could not count. It was not loneliness. It was something quieter and much more patient: the sense of a ferry hauled up on the bank for the winter, tarred and ready, with every seam sound, and nobody standing on the far shore.

She stood there until the cold got into her gloves. Then she turned and walked back up the mole and along the path, and the steps came out right, as they always did. Sixty-three to the house. Behind her, nine stairs up to the loft.

In the kitchen window above the shed, her father's light stayed on a long time. She did not look up to see why. She had learned to leave that alone, too.

###CHAPTER 3: What the Wood Remembers

By Wednesday the putty had set, and the sash needed a fastener.

The original was long gone. Sometime in the nineteen-fifties a person with no respect for the frame had screwed a flat modern catch across the meeting rail, and it had to come off. The house on the ridge road wanted a brass sash lock of the right period, and the right period was not something you could buy. It was something you had.

Nora had.

The back wall of the shed was drawers. There were forty-odd of them, salvaged from kitchens and shops and parlors that had been pulled down or gutted: a chemist's wall of tiny ones, the lower half of a ship chandler's cabinet, a dozen that had once held somebody's linens. She called it the keeping wall. Each drawer held what someone had been about to throw away, and each was a small argument against waste. She knew where everything was. She had never made a list. Wood remembered everything, every cut and dent and spilled cup, which was one of the reasons she trusted it. People were more careful. People remembered selectively.

Third row, fourth from the left: fasteners. She slid the drawer out, and the brass lay in it in a dull heap, rubbed smooth by other people's thumbs. She sorted with her fingertips, not looking, and under the heap, at the very back, her hand closed on a bundle in a handkerchief she had long ago stopped seeing.

She knew what it was without unwrapping it. She unwrapped it anyway.

It was a sash lock, small and plain and worn bright at the lever, and it was the first thing she had ever mended.

Hannah had lived in the top room of a student house on a hill, in a window that would never quite close, and in the winter of their first year Nora had come up the stairs with a borrowed screwdriver and fixed it, for no reason except that it was wrong. Hannah had watched her do it with her chin on her fist. She had been twenty-one, bright and fast, the kind of person who could walk into a room and make it sit up, and she had taken Nora's silences for depth. For two years Nora had been, as far as Hannah was concerned, profound. It had been wonderful and slightly terrifying, like holding a very fine glass that someone had filled to the brim.

At the end of the second year Hannah got a place abroad. She had told Nora about it on the stairs, at night, with her bag already half-packed and her face lit up like a window, and then she had stopped talking and waited. Nora had known exactly what the waiting was for. She had understood it completely, the way you understand the outline of a thing in the dark. And she had said, with her whole heart, "Of course you should go. It's what you've always wanted."

Hannah had looked at her a long moment, as though there were another sentence coming. When it didn't, she picked up her bag.

Nora had taken the lock off the window the week the house was sold, and kept it, and told no one. She turned it over now in her palm. It was a good lock. It still worked perfectly. She wrapped it up again and put it back at the bottom of the drawer, under the others, where it belonged. Some things were not spare parts.

From the heap she chose a plain brass catch from a house she did not remember, fitted it against the rail, and it was right at once, the way a thing is right when it has been waiting a long time to be asked.

She drove the screws, and she hummed, and did not hear herself.

Lower down the wall, at about the height of her knee, was the drawer she tried not to look at. It was blue. It had been blue on the day it was made, and the shade had worn at the edges to the color of a tired sky. Maren had painted it, in the first year, with a tin of paint that was meant for the bathroom door, and had stood back and said it looked cheerful. It was the only drawer in the shed she hadn't rescued from a demolition. It had come out of the kitchen table they had shared for five years.

Five good years. She would say that to anyone. Warm, and kind, and easy, a house where the radiators knocked on the same schedule as the kettle. Maren had made her laugh every day, and Nora had mended every hinge in the place, and if two people could be said to have loved each other well, they had.

It had gone out slowly and gently, the way a fire goes out when no one is feeding it, and no one was to blame. On the last evening they sat in the kitchen with the table between them and a pot of tea neither of them wanted, and Maren, who never raised her voice, had said, very quietly, "You never asked me for anything, Nora. Not once in five years. Do you know how lonely that is, to love someone who needs nothing?"

"I don't need anything," Nora had said. She had meant it kindly.

Maren had laughed, a small tired sound, and put her hand over her eyes. "I know," she said. "That's what I mean."

At the time Nora had filed it under compliments. It had taken her most of two years to take it out again and look at it, and even now she wasn't sure she had understood it, or that she wanted to. She had taken the drawer when she left, because Maren put it in her hands and said, "You made it. Take it." It held glazing points now, and tacks, and a chipped blue enamel button. She put her hand flat on its front for a moment, the way you lay a hand on the side of an animal to feel it breathe. Then she took the hand away.

The winter was different. The winter was the one with the rule in it.

Her name was Tessa. Nora had stopped saying it, which was not the same as forgetting it. She had come to the shed in October, thirty-one and freshly out, to herself and to almost no one else, with a bay window that wouldn't keep a draft out and a question she did not yet know how to put. Nora mended the window. By Christmas she was mending a good deal more.

It had been, as far as Nora could tell, the real thing. That was what she could never get around. Tessa had come to her bed that December with her hands shaking and her eyes enormous, and had said, "I've never done this. I don't know what I'm doing," and Nora had said, "Neither of us has to know," and meant it, and for three months she had known only that. She went slowly. She asked before she touched. She sat up all one night in January while Tessa cried after telephoning her mother, and held her until the crying turned to a long, exhausted laughter, and in the morning made her eggs. She answered questions that no one had ever asked her, patiently, honestly, in the dark: what it was like, whether it got easier, whether Tessa was allowed to want this much. She did not think of it as teaching. She would have been appalled to hear it called that. She thought of it as being there, which was the thing she did best, and she did it with the whole of her heart, because somewhere in the second month the whole of her heart had quietly come along.

Nobody had said what it was. That was all. Nobody had said <i>this is a winter</i>, or <i>this is a place for me to learn</i>, or <i>I will go when I know what I want</i>, so that Nora could have said yes to it, or no. Tessa had not hidden it. Tessa hadn't known it herself.

In March they sat on the mole in the thin sun with a flask of coffee, and Tessa told her about a woman she had met at a gallery, someone her own age, someone who had known who she was for years. She was lit up when she said it. Her eyes were wet. She took Nora's hand in both of hers and said, "I don't know what I would have done without you. You were exactly what I needed this winter."

<i>This winter.</i> It was a lovely sentence. It was a sentence you could have embroidered. And Nora had sat there in the sun and felt the whole shape of the thing come clear at once, the way a window comes clear when the last coat of old paint is stripped away: not a love, not a mistake, but a season in somebody else's life, with a beginning and an end that had been decided without her. She had said, "I'm so glad." She had meant that too. Nobody had been cruel. That was the worst of it. There was no one to forgive.

That night at the kitchen sink, with her hands in the water, she had made her rule out loud to a stack of plates: <i>not as a first, and not as a lesson. Never again.</i>

There had been a card, of course. It came the following week with a drawing of a bird on the front, and inside, in a round hand: <i>Thank you for being so patient with me. You taught me what I wanted. I'll never forget this winter.</i> Nora had read it standing up, and put it in the cleft of the beam above the shed door, where the old ferrymen had kept their flood tables, to be rid of it. She had not been able to throw it away. It was still up there. It was, she sometimes thought, the truest thing she knew about the whole business: that a person could be thanked so warmly, and so completely, by someone who was already gone.

She wiped her hands on her apron and went back to the window.

At four o'clock the kitchen window above the shed opened.

It did this every Wednesday, a minute or two before the telephone. Her father aired the kitchen as though for company. Then came the ring, one and a half, never more, because he always had the receiver up before the second, and then his voice.

Nora had stopped trying not to hear it years ago. The shed was directly beneath the kitchen, and sound came down through the old boards like water through sand. She heard what she had always heard: a voice she knew and did not. It was easy. It ran on. It had long sentences in it, with rises and falls, with places where it stopped to laugh, and the laugh was a low, startled, helpless thing that she had heard from him perhaps four times in her life face to face. This was the same man who could not find words for lentils. On Wednesdays at four he found them by the dozen.

She never caught the words. She did not try. She knew only the music, and the music was this: a man talking to someone who had known him for forty years and could finish every sentence of his and nevertheless wanted to hear them said. Twelve miles of telephone wire lay between this kitchen and her mother's apartment in town, and her father's voice crossed them without a hesitation, as though it were nothing. As though water were not a thing that needed carrying at all.

Once, in the middle of it, she heard her own name. She stood with the screwdriver in her hand until it went by.

The call lasted nineteen minutes, which she did not count. She counted nothing at all. At the end the window closed. A minute after that, she heard the back door and the slow steps on the path, which was not his hour, and he was standing in the shed doorway with his hands at his sides and nothing in them.

"Your mother says the doorstop is her favorite."

"She says that every time."

"She says Sunday."

"I know."

"Good."

He stood a moment longer than the message required. There was still something left on him from the call, a loosened thing around the mouth and eyes, like light on a wall after the lamp has been taken out of the room. Nora, who looked at hands first, looked at his face instead, for once, and without counting anything. For a moment he looked like a man who might say anything at all.

Then he nodded, and went back up the path.

She turned back to the window. It closed now, smooth and square, on its brass lock from a house she did not remember, and she stood and looked at it for some time before she realized that she had been waiting for something, and that nothing had come, and that she was not, for once, entirely sure it had been the water.

###CHAPTER 4: Eleven Steps

The first frost came on the last Thursday of October, and Ines was as good as her word.

She arrived on Friday at noon in the car that announced itself, and she came down the path with nothing in her hands. That was how Nora knew it was serious.

"There's a place in the city," Ines said, settling onto the crate by the door. "Saturday. I'm not asking."

"You're asking."

"I'm telling you that I'm asking. It's different. You don't have to talk to anyone. You can stand at the bar and look at hands. You can make a close study of the woodwork. But you'll be there, and you'll be there in the navy shirt." She considered Nora over the rim of an imaginary cup. "I have been asking since September. I ask the way you wait. It has worn a groove."

Nora looked past her at the river. Overnight the frost had laid a gray bloom on the stones of the dry bed, and the whole long, bared floor of it glittered where the sun reached. Something in her wanted very much to say no, and she knew exactly what that something was, and where it lived, and what it had cost. She had a rule. Ines knew about the rule, in the way she knew about the weather: you didn't mention it, you dressed for it.

"One drink," Nora said.

"Four hours," said Ines, "and no one is counting."

"You're counting."

"I'm always counting. I learned from the best."

The club was in the old part of the city, up a flight of stairs that smelled of other people's coats, and it was exactly as loud as Nora had remembered and a great deal warmer. The bass came up through the floorboards. She had always rather respected a good floor: forty years of dancing had given this one a faint, forgiving give, like a boat. She stood with her back to the bar and read the room as she would have read a house with its plaster off. Where the weight was. Where the damp had come in.

Ines was telling her about the dishwasher. This was the second installment, the one involving a lasagna dish that had gone in on Tuesday and come out, in a state of grace, on Thursday, and Ines was halfway through a long and affectionate indictment of her brother-in-law when Nora stopped listening.

It was not that the story was dull. It was that a woman had come in from the edge of the room and stopped at the bar with a drink she was not drinking, and Nora had noticed her hands first, as she always did. They were wrapped around the glass the way you wrap them around a warm cup on a cold porch. Then the rest of her arrived: auburn hair, loosened from some careful pinning; a simple dress, put on with great care; eyes that moved across the dancers the way a person reads a language she has only lately learned to speak.

<i>Careful,</i> Nora thought. <i>Not a girl. Old enough to know exactly why she came. New enough to wonder whether she's allowed.</i>

And something else, which she could not have named and did not try to: a way of standing as though her body had recently told her something true, and she was still learning where to put it.

"You've gone quiet," Ines said. "Either I'm finally boring you, or someone's walked in."

"The dishwasher is a tragedy. I'm following it closely."

"Mm. Go over there. You look like you're waiting for a ferry."

Nora laughed, but she did not move. October had been a long month. She knew what the rule was for. She knew exactly how a person looked in October, new and frightened and shining, in the doorway of a shed with a window that wouldn't keep the draft out. This woman was every inch the same, and Nora felt the old caution close around her ribs like a hand.

And yet.

Across the room the woman looked up and met her eyes, and she did not look away. That was what loosened the rule: not the beauty, which was real, but the nerve. She was frightened, anyone could see it, and she stayed. <i>Someone who stays when she's afraid,</i> Nora thought, <i>is worth walking across a room for.</i>

She would ask questions first. She would be plain. She could do plain.

"Back in a minute," she told Ines, who raised her glass to the ceiling in a toast to her own wisdom.

Nora set off across the floor. She counted. She always counted: a thing that had been counted could not surprise you. One, two, the bass in her teeth. Three, four, a woman in green laughing with her whole throat. Five, six. Seven.

At seven, she lost the count.

She would think about that later, for a long time. It had never happened to her before, not in thirty-two years, not at a graveside, not at the top of the loft ladder in a gale. She simply arrived: at the bar, at the edge of a smile, with no idea how far she had come.

"Hello," she said. "I'm Nora. My friend says I look like I'm waiting for a ferry."

The woman laughed, a low, surprised sound, as if it had been waiting some time to be let out. "Sophie. Is it running late?"

"It doesn't run until April. I'm very patient."

They talked about the music, which was too loud; about the bartender, who was clearly a genius; about the odd bravery of walking up to a stranger in a room full of people. Sophie said she had not been in a place like this before.

"Not here," she said. "Not like this."

Nora heard the <i>not like this</i>, and what lay behind it, and left it alone. <i>Not mine to ask yet. If it matters, she'll tell me.</i> And under that, quieter: <i>If it's a bridge, I'll know by the way she says goodbye.</i>

She asked questions. She asked what Sophie did, and listened to the answer with her whole face, as though it were the only answer in the room. She asked about the long drive. She asked nothing at all about the thing behind <i>not like this</i>. She watched Sophie's hands, which had begun to let go of the glass, and then her shoulders, which came down from around her ears one inch at a time, like a house settling in the night. At some point Sophie laughed three times in a row and looked startled, as if she had found money in a coat.

Near eleven the room had warmed, and the music had gone dark and slow, and Sophie's drink stood nearly full on the bar. Nora said, "I'd like to kiss you. You can say no, and I'll walk you to your car and think very well of you. Or you can say yes, and tell me to slow down whenever you like."

She watched the fear cross Sophie's face and rise, and watched Sophie, with visible effort, let it pass through her like weather.

"Yes," Sophie said. "Slowly."

"I'm good at slowly," said Nora. "It's most of my trade."

The kiss was careful, and then it was not. And in it Nora felt something she had not felt in years: not the thrill of being first, nor the comfort of being safe, but the quiet click of a well-cut joint, two edges that fit.

<i>Oh,</i> she thought. <i>Well. There you are.</i>

When they drew apart, Sophie's eyes were open and wet and very clear. She did not seem to know what to do with her hands, and Nora took one of them, because it was there, and turned it palm up, because she always looked at hands, and held it.

"I'd like to see you again," Nora said.

And she waited.

It was only a second. It was the second she had spent two years learning to dread: the one before the other person said the sentence, the pretty one, the one with the word in it. <i>Thank you.</i> <i>Thank you so much.</i> The word that meant <i>you were exactly what I needed</i>, and opened a door that closed on the far side.

"I'd like that too," said Sophie.

That was all. The word did not come. She did not thank her, for the kiss, or the evening, or for being kind. She only borrowed a pen from the bartender, and took Nora's hand again, and wrote her number on the inside of the wrist in a small, steady, slightly ridiculous hand, as if she were making an entry in a ledger she intended to keep.

It was a very small thing. It was, Nora understood, the whole thing.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Ines drove her home. For the first twenty minutes she said nothing at all, which was the most remarkable thing she had done in fourteen years, and Nora sat in the passenger seat, with her wrist turned face-up on her knee in the passing lights, and let herself be driven.

"Eleven," Ines said eventually.

"What?"

"Steps. From where we were to the bar. I counted."

"I lost count at seven."

"I know," said Ines, eyes on the road. "That's why I counted."

Nora looked out at the dark fields going by. After a while she realized that she had been humming, and that Ines, who had known her for fourteen years, had said nothing, and would say nothing, because there are things you do not tell a person while she is doing them.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

It was past one when she got back to the shed. The night was clear and bitterly cold, and the frost had already begun to whiten the path. She stopped under the beam above the door, where a small square of card lay folded in the old ferrymen's cleft, and looked up at it for a while.

She did not take it down. She did not need to. It had stopped being a warning, somewhere in the last two hours, and become a kind of measure, like the pencil marks on the frame: something to check the water against.

She took out the stub of pencil she kept on the sill, and went to read the day's level, as she did every night, out of habit, even though it was past midnight and already tomorrow.

The river had come up an inch.

She stood and looked at the mark for a long time. It had rained in the hills, she supposed, somewhere she would never see. All the marks on the frame, from the first one in the oldest hand, went down. October was the month for it. She had never, in thirty-two years, written one that went the other way.

She wrote it at the top of a new column, above the others, and dated it, and went up the nine stairs to bed.

###CHAPTER 5: Slowly

Nora had always been good at waiting. It turned out she was good at it only when nothing was on the other end.

She did not wash her left wrist for a day and a half. Then, being a person who washed, she copied the number into the back of the glazing ledger in pencil, where it would last, and put the telephone in her apron pocket, and took it out, and put it back, and was appalled.

Three days, she had decided. Three days was the right interval for a person like Sophie: long enough not to press, short enough not to seem to be waiting. She lasted until Monday at four.

"It's Nora," she said.

"I know. You're in my phone." A pause, and the low surprised laugh, a little breathless. "I mean, I put you in. After. In the car."

"You wrote yours on my wrist."

"I did. It was a very forward thing to do."

"It's also in the glazing ledger. In pencil." She closed her eyes. "I'm not sure why I told you that."

"I'm very glad you did," said Sophie. "Do you want to go for a walk?"

On Tuesday morning her father stood at the top of the mole with his hands behind his back and said, to the river, "Four inches since Friday."

"I saw."

"Good." He looked at the water for ten seconds. Then he looked at the shed for thirty-five, and at the shape of the telephone in the pocket of her apron, and, for the first time in her memory, at the end of it, he very nearly smiled.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They walked upriver on Thursday, on the old towpath, in a thin cold sun that made the bare willows look lacquered. Nora named things. The heron that stood in the shallows like a coat left on a hook; the broken lock where the water spilled over green stone; the place where the otters came down in spring. She did not take her to the landing, or the shed, or the ferry sleeping on its cradle. They passed within a quarter mile of them, on the far bank, and Sophie shaded her eyes and said, "What's that?" and Nora said, "That's where I work," and kept walking.

It was not a lie, and it was not the whole truth, and she was aware of exactly how much of each it was. There would be a day for the shed. It was too much of her to hand over on the first walk. It was the one place she had never been a lesson in.

"Where does it go?" Sophie asked, nodding at the river.

"Down to the sea, in the end. I've never followed it."

"Why not?"

"I've always been the one who stays where the crossing is." She heard how that sounded and shrugged. "Someone has to."

Sophie walked a few steps in silence. Then she said, "I think I've been the opposite. All crossing. I never stayed on either side long enough to find out what it was like."

It was the most honest thing anyone had said to Nora in a year, and it had been said to a heron. She did not turn her head. She said, "What's it like, so far?" and Sophie said, "Cold, mostly," and they both laughed, and their hands, which had been swinging, found one another without anyone deciding it.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

On Saturday they had dinner in a small place with eleven tables and candles in jam jars, and the talk moved without anyone steering it from light things into more personal ground. Nora told her about her father and the mug on the mole. She had not told anyone that in years. She had no idea why she was telling it now, except that Sophie listened as if every word were the first of its kind, and let the silences stand.

Sophie told her about her mother. "She's a gardener. Mara. She's the bravest person I know." And then, a moment later, in a voice she did not seem to know she had changed, "My mother and her wife," and her whole face altered around the word <i>wife</i>, the way a room changes when a lamp goes on in the next one.

Nora noticed. She noticed what did not follow it, too, like a patch of unpainted wall in a freshly done house. <i>Not mine to ask,</i> she thought. <i>If it matters, she'll tell me.</i>

"Who came before me?" Sophie asked, abruptly, as if she had been working up to it with the bread.

It was a fair question. Nora set down her fork. "Hannah. Two years, when we were very young. And Maren, five years, a good one, and then a quiet one. Both good people. Both left without anyone doing anything wrong." She hesitated. "There's one more. A winter. I'm not ready to tell it. I'm telling you that so you know it's a shut door, and not a missing wall."

She watched Sophie's face, and found nothing in it that she had been braced for.

"I'd rather have a shut door than a painted-over one," Sophie said slowly. "That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me on a second date."

"Third," said Nora. "The walk counts."

"Third," Sophie agreed, and her hand came across the candle and found Nora's wrist, the very place, with the thumb on the pulse, as if checking it was still there.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Nora's apartment was a single room over a hardware store in town, twenty-two steps up from the street, and she had kept it for six years after Maren, for clients who wanted to see samples, and for the nights when the loft by the river was too cold for any sane person. It was bare, and clean, and smelled of beeswax. It had one good chair and one good window, and she had restored both.

She counted the steps going up, out of habit. Behind her, Sophie said, "You're counting."

"Twenty-two."

"Is it always twenty-two?"

"It has been every time."

"I like that," said Sophie, so lightly that Nora almost missed that nobody had ever said it before.

They went slowly. Nora asked before she touched, and asked again, and every time Sophie said yes it was a slightly different yes: a tentative one, a surprised one, then a low steady one with something under it like a held note. At one point Sophie's breath caught and she went very still, the way people do at the edge of something, and Nora, who had been listening for it, did not move and did not ask if she was all right. She only stayed, with one hand flat on the curve of Sophie's back, until the thing went through her like weather. And Sophie, astonishingly, stayed too. She did not reach for distance. She lay with her face against Nora's neck and her whole weight upon her, like someone who had put down something heavy and did not intend to pick it up, and Nora lay under it and listened to her breathe.

She did not sleep for a long time. She watched the light from the street move across the ceiling, and in the dark a quiet, private arithmetic began in her, which she did not name, and which she would have been ashamed to admit.

She was counting the days since the word that hadn't come.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Six nights became ten. The towpath became the shed's far bank and then, one Sunday, the shed itself, though that is another story. The river rose a little every day, a few inches here, a hand's breadth there, and her father reported it to the water each morning in the same flat voice.

Nora counted. She told herself it was only habit. Day eleven: Sophie said she liked the bread. Day fourteen: Sophie cried at a song on the radio and laughed at herself. Day seventeen: Sophie fell asleep in the one good chair, with her feet on a crate of old window weights, and Nora covered her with a blanket and stood in the middle of her own room, unmoving, for a very long time.

Day nineteen. It was raining. It had been raining since noon, steadily, with a sound like someone sorting small stones, and Sophie lay on her back in the dark with her eyes open and the tension in her that Nora had learned to read the way she read the grain in oak.

"There's something I need to tell you," Sophie said. "About the last couple of months. About how I got here."

Nora turned toward her, eyes open and patient. Somewhere inside, though she did not know it yet, the count stopped.

Sophie told her. She did it carefully and plainly, the way you carry something full to the brim across a room: the pattern of shallow, brief affairs; the day she had gone home to her mother's house with her question; what Lena had offered, and what Mara had known, and how each of them had said, from the first, what it was and what it was for. A mentoring. A few weeks. Real in the moment and not meant to last. Lena had called it a <i>bridge</i>.

The word went through Nora like a draft through a bad window.

For a second the room tilted, the way a floor tilts when you step on a board you thought was sound. <i>A bridge.</i> She heard the dry sound of the card in its cleft above the shed door. <i>You taught me what I wanted.</i> She waited, as she had waited a hundred times, for the sentence to turn and show its far side.

It did not turn. There was no far side. Sophie had put the whole of it into her hands, front and back, and lay beside her, white to the lips, waiting for what Nora would do with it.

And that was the difference, Nora understood, slowly, the way you understand weather. No one had told her about Tessa's winter. Tessa herself had not known. She had learned its name in March, in the thin sun, over coffee, as a thing already finished. Sophie was telling her in November, with Nora's heart still wide open, with her own heart going so hard that Nora could feel it through the sheet. She was being given the word <i>before</i> the door closed. She was being given the chance to say yes, or no.

She searched herself for the old sting. It was there. She would have been worried if it were not. But it had a different shape now, not <i>I have been used</i> but <i>I have been told</i>, and the second was a thing she had never had in her hands before. It felt, strangely, like being trusted with a key.

"Thank you for telling me," she said quietly.

She heard herself say it. It was the word. It was the very same word, and it was not at all the same, because there was no door behind it. She meant it all the way through.

"It sounds like they gave you something important," she went on. "And it sounds like you understood what it was for." She stopped, and then, because it was true, "I'm glad you're not keeping half of yourself back with me. I can feel the difference."

Sophie's face broke, and she pulled Nora toward her and held on, hard. And Nora, who had spent the better part of her life being the arm other people leaned on, shut her eyes and let herself be held in return. It took some doing. Arms are a thing you have to learn to be inside.

She did not tell her about the winter. It was not the night for it. It was Sophie's night, and she had earned all of it, and Nora meant to take it the way you take a gift when you know the whole cost: slowly, with both hands, without pretending it was nothing. There would be a night for hers. For the first time, she thought there might be.

Somewhere in the dark, in that long rain, the count she had kept for nineteen days ended, and she could not have said when.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

She drove down to the landing on Thursday afternoon, in a gray light that smelled of wet leaves and woodsmoke. The river had come up past the second stone and was running brown and quick and talkative along the mole.

She had not been at the shed for two nights, and she walked up to the doorframe with the old reflex, pencil already in her fingers, ready to write in the numbers she had missed.

They were there.

In her column, the new one at the top, above the downward marks of the autumn, there were two entries, and neither was in her hand. They were square, and slanting, and slightly crooked, and each of them was dated. <i>Tuesday. Wednesday.</i> He had read the water for her. He had not said a word.

She stood for a long moment with the pencil in her hand.

Then she wrote the day's mark beneath his, in the same column, in her own hand, and felt, absurdly, a little less alone, and a good deal more carried, than she would have thought a few inches of pencil could make a person feel.

###CHAPTER 6: The Window Sash

Nora could lift almost anything. It had never occurred to her that this was a different thing from having to.

The sash from the old schoolhouse had stood against the back wall of the shed since August: nine feet of heart pine, twelve panes of glass poured in the 1880s that still moved when you moved your head, and heavy in the way of wood that has been soaked and dried a hundred and forty times. She had spent four months on it. She had scraped it, re-pinned it, glazed it, hung it with new cord. All that was left was to lift it into the long frame in the south wall of the shed, where every big sash hung for a week before anyone trusted it, and hook it to its weights.

It was a two-person job. She had done it alone twice. It took forty minutes and a block and tackle slung from the beam, a ratchet strap, six wedges, and a great deal of cleverness, and at the end of it she was always damp and pleased, in the particular way of a person who has proved a point to no one.

Ines had offered, every time. "I'm not made of porcelain, Nora. Hand me the end of the thing." Her father's back had gone in the winter of the ice, and he did not offer, because he knew better. Maren had held a sash for her once, years ago, and Nora had thanked her for a week, until Maren said, with a hand against her cheek, "Stop paying me."

She had not understood that either. All she knew was that a thing carried for you was a thing owed, and that it was better to carry it yourself and owe no one.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Sophie had seen the shed once, on a Sunday in November. Nora had braced for it the way you brace for the dentist, and it had turned out to be nothing like that. Sophie had not said <i>charming</i>. She had not said anything. She had walked slowly along the keeping wall with her hands clasped behind her back, like someone in a museum, reading labels that were not there, and had stopped for a moment under the beam above the door, for no reason either of them named. Nora's breath had stopped with her. Then Sophie had walked on and asked what the lathe was for, and Nora's lungs had resumed, and neither of them mentioned it.

Since then she had come on Saturdays.

She came now at ten, on a gray, bitter morning in the first week of December, stamping the cold off her boots, with two coffees and a paper bag that smelled of cardamom. Outside, the river stood at its winter height, brown and glossy and silently fast, with a rim of ice along the edge of the mole.

She stopped in the doorway. Nora had already hung the block from the beam. The strap was threaded. The wedges lay in a row on the bench like instruments.

"That's a lot of rope," Sophie said.

"It's a nine-foot sash. It's a lot of window."

"Do you need—" Sophie stopped herself so fast that Nora felt it as a small physical check, like a hand drawn back from a stove. She said, lightly, "Tell me where to put the coffee."

Nora looked at the rope.

The sentence was already in her mouth, and it was the old sentence, the smooth one, worn to its shape by long use: <i>I've got it.</i> She had said it to Ines, to Maren, to Hannah, to every person who had ever stood in a doorway with a free pair of hands. It cost nothing. That was its whole virtue. In thirty-two years it had never once cost her anything at all, and she thought, with the clarity of a person at the top of a dark stair, <i>and look what it has bought.</i>

"I could do this myself," she said.

Sophie said nothing. She stood very still with a coffee in each hand.

"I've done it myself twice. It works."

"I believe you."

"I'd just rather not."

It came out smaller than she had meant and landed on the floor with an audible sound. She had expected the room to tilt. It did not. It stayed exactly where it was, with its linseed and its lathe and its thin gray light, and in the middle of it Sophie set the two coffees on the bench, very carefully, one and then the other, as if they might go off.

"Which end?" she said.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"The low one. Hands here, and here." Nora's voice came out almost normal. "When I say three, we lift, and then <i>in</i>. Not forward. It'll want to come at you. You let it come as far as your chest and no farther. It's heavier than it looks."

"Everything you make is heavier than it looks."

"It's the glass."

They lifted on three. It was heavier than it looked. For a moment the whole weight of the thing hung between them, and Nora felt it run along the pine and through the old glass and into Sophie's hands, and felt, quite distinctly, the instant at which her own arms discovered that they were not carrying all of it. It was a strange sensation, very like vertigo: a floor that wasn't where she'd left it. "In," she said, and they walked it in, and the sash found its track as though it had been homesick. The cords took it. Inside the wall the weights rose and settled with a deep wooden sound, like a breath let go.

It hung. It was perfectly plumb. Neither of them moved.

Then Nora looked across, because she looked at hands first, and saw that Sophie's were trembling.

"You're shaking."

"It's not the weight."

"What is it?"

"You asked me." Sophie laughed, a little wildly, and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. "I know it wasn't much. I know it's a window. But I stood in that doorway and I could see you deciding, and I thought, <i>please</i>, and I was trying so hard not to want it." She dropped her hand. "I'm so glad I could burst."

"You're allowed to say that."

"I'm trying to say it with some dignity."

"Fail," said Nora, and heard her own laugh come out, rusty and astonished, and a moment later she had crossed the floor and had both arms around her, and they stood like that in the cold shed in front of the nine-foot window.

"Thank you," she said into Sophie's hair, and waited, out of long habit, for the click of a door.

"Anytime," said Sophie.

It was an astonishing thing to hear. It had a door in it, and the door opened inward.

Nora drew back. "Now you."

"Now me what?"

"Ask me for something. Anything. Small." She meant it, and was dismayed to find that she meant it with a little of the nervousness she had felt on the other side of the exchange. "I don't want to be the only one who gets carried."

Sophie thought about it, which was in itself something to see. A flush went up from her collar. "Teach me to sharpen a chisel," she said. "Properly. The way you do, with your eyes half shut. I watched you last Sunday, and I thought I'd give a great deal to know what you know in your hands."

"That's a week's work. Longer."

"I have a week."

"It's boring."

"Then you'll have to be patient with me," said Sophie, and Nora, who had been patient with a great many people through a great many winters, heard the sentence differently this time. She said, "Tuesday," before she had decided to, and found that she meant it with her whole heart, which was something she would have to think about later.

She had not counted anything for twenty minutes. She noticed this only afterwards.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Her father came down at noon, as he did whenever the river stood above the second stone, with the report already in his face and a mug in each hand.

"Eleven inches since Sunday week," he told the shed. "Ice at the edges."

"I saw."

"Good." His eye went to the long window, to the sash hanging straight in its frame, and stayed there. He walked up to it and laid the flat of his hand on the rail, the way he did on a gunwale after a crossing, and ran it down the stile. "Plumb," he said.

It was the best thing he had said about her work in twenty years. It hit her under the ribs, and she had no idea what to do with her face.

"Who set it?"

"We did."

"We," he said, quietly, to himself, as a man tests a plank.

"This is Sophie."

He turned. She was ready for the handshake and the nod. Instead she watched him do what she had thought was hers alone: he looked at Sophie's hands. The cold-reddened knuckles, the fingers not quite steady yet. It took perhaps three seconds. Then he held out the second mug.

"It's tea," he said. "I don't know how you take it."

"As it comes." Sophie wrapped both hands around it and drank, and her eyes closed briefly. "Oh. That's exactly right."

He grunted, which was the sound he made instead of a smile, and looked out at the river. "The lane's under at the low place," he said, to it. "By the poplars."

"I know. I'll go round by the church road."

Something went between them then, no longer than a held breath, across Nora like a wire pulled taut, and was gone. She had never taken Sophie by the church road. They always came by the lane. She noted this the way she noted a board a quarter inch off, and filed it where she filed things that were not hers to ask, a place with a great deal of room in it. Sophie had learned the district fast. That was all. Her father was a man who said things to rivers.

He stayed a moment longer than the report required, with his hand still flat on the rail, as though the wood were warm. Then he went back up the path.

"He said <i>plumb</i>," said Sophie, when he was gone. "That's a sonnet."

"He doesn't know what to say."

"That's what I mean," said Sophie, and Nora thought of Maren on a night years ago, and for once did not look away from the thought.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They ate cardamom buns on the bench in the middle of the afternoon with the wedges pushed to one side. Sophie held a chisel for the first time, wrongly, and was told so, and held it again. In the late light, as she was pulling on her coat, she stood a moment in the door with her mouth a little open on a sentence, and Nora, watching her, saw something cross her face and be put away, carefully, like a gift kept for later.

"Tuesday," Sophie said instead.

When the car had gone, Nora stood alone in the shed. The cloud had thinned for ten minutes, and a low yellow light lay across the floor and climbed the nine-foot window. On the other side of the glass the river was moving as it always did. But the old pane did to it what the old panes did: it bulged and stretched and gathered, so that the water seemed to be thinking about itself. She had hung a hundred such windows. It was the first time she had noticed what they did. They did not show you the river. They showed you the river as someone had once seen it.

She went to the doorframe and wrote the day's mark in her column. Beneath it, in the crooked square hand, there was already another. <i>Saturday.</i> She had not seen him come.

She had asked for something, and nothing had been taken from her. That was the part she turned over all that evening, with the gentleness you use on a thing you have found and are not sure you may keep. She had thought a request would cost her. It had cost her one sentence. And the sentence had been the part she was afraid of; the rest had only been a window.

###CHAPTER 7: Hannah

In the third week of March the river gave things back.

The ice went out on a Tuesday at four in the afternoon, with a sound like a great door being dragged across a stone floor, and for three days afterward everything that had been lying under it came up and went by: branches, a drowned fence post, a blue boat cushion, the bleached rib of something that might once have been a rowing seat. On Thursday it brought down a window. Only the frame, no glass and no sash, four mitred corners of black oak gone soft and silver. Her father hooked it out of the shallows with the boat hook and stood it against the wall of the shed.

"Somebody's," he said.

"Whose?"

"Somebody's." As though that were an answer, and it was. "Nineteen inches since Monday." He looked at the water, brown and fast and full of itself. "She's in a hurry."

Nora looked at the frame for some time after he had gone. It was a thing worth thinking about, how much a river could hold down, and for how long, and how little warning it gave.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

The letter came on Friday, in the tin box at the head of the lane. It was a single sheet, folded in three, and she knew the writing before she had the box shut: fast, slanting, every <i>t</i> crossed with one stroke like a person throwing something away.

<i>Nora,</i>

<i>I'm in town until the fifteenth, clearing my father's house on Linden Street. The windows are original and I would like someone who knows to tell me whether they can be saved.</i>

<i>That's partly true. The other part is that I've been writing this letter since I was twenty-three, and this is the shortest version.</i>

<i>Hannah</i>

She read it standing up, as she read everything that mattered. She had once read a card that way too, and had put it in the cleft above the shed door to be rid of it. She stood now under the same beam, with the paper in her hand, and did not reach up. She folded it in four and put it in her apron pocket, beside the telephone, where it lay all afternoon against her hip like a small flat stone.

That evening she told Sophie. She had not planned to. They were washing up in the room over the hardware store, and Nora, drying a plate that was already dry, said, "Hannah wrote to me."

Sophie set the pan down very slowly. She knew the name. It had come up at dinner in November, between the bread and the shut door. "What does she want?"

"A window looked at. And something else."

"Do you want to see her?"

"I think I have to."

"That's not the same as wanting."

"No."

Sophie dried her hands. She took her time about it. "Do you want me to come? Or do you want me far away?"

Nora gave it an honest turn, the way she would have given a joint, and found no answer in it. "I don't know."

"That's allowed," said Sophie. And then, with the care of a person laying down something she has chosen not to hide: "Can I ask you for one thing?"

Nora's hands went still on the plate. "Yes."

"Call me after. Not because I need anything. Because I'd like to know you're all right. And because I'd like to be the one you tell."

It was an easy thing to give, and a strange one to receive. It asked for nothing but her.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Linden Street ran up the shoulder of the hill behind the old town, a row of narrow brick houses with green doors and iron boot scrapers and gardens like folded handkerchiefs. The house was number nine. There were fourteen steps from the gate to the door, and Nora counted eleven of them and lost the thread. She stood on the top step, a little appalled, and the door opened before she could knock.

Eleven years had done to Hannah what they do to most people who are loved: they had taken the brightness off her, the lamp-on-in-a-window quality, and left something steadier behind. A streak of gray ran back from one temple. The quickness had gone into her hands. They were doing three things at once, holding a roll of packing tape and a mug and a scrap of newspaper, and they stopped doing any of them when she saw Nora.

"You're looking at my hands," said Hannah.

"I always do."

"I know. I used to think it meant you were shy." She smiled, and it was the same smile, with less to prove. "It meant you were listening."

Neither of them knew what to do next. Then Hannah said, "The windows are upstairs," and Nora said, "Show me," and for a quarter of an hour they were two professionals in a hallway, which was a mercy.

There were nine sashes, and Nora took them in order. She laid her palm flat on each pane, put her thumbnail into the putty, listened to the cord with her ear against the stile. The ones in the kitchen were sound. The ones on the stair had gone, a little, along the sill. In the back bedroom on the top floor, a narrow room under the roof with a bed made up in a faded blue quilt, the window had been painted shut by someone who loved it, five or six coats, until the sash and the frame were one piece, and she stood a long time with her hand against it.

"This is yours," she said. It was not a question.

"It was my room. I was born in it, more or less." Hannah leaned in the door. "Can it be saved?"

"All of it can. Pine. The rope's gone and the pulleys are seized, and it's wanted opening for forty years. Three weeks. Maybe four." She took her hand away from the glass. "It's a good window."

"You haven't changed," said Hannah, and laughed. "I write you the most terrible letter I've ever written, and you come up the hill and give me a quotation."

"You wrote about windows."

"I wrote about windows."

"Then I'm answering about windows."

Hannah laughed again, and it went wrong in the middle. She sat down hard on the edge of the bed, in the blue quilt, with the packing tape still in her hand, and said, "Oh, Nora," and then, after a while, "Sit down. Please. There's a chair. I'll say it badly if I stand."

Nora sat.

"I'm happy," said Hannah. "I want that said first, so you don't think I've come to be rescued. Her name is Ilse. She stayed home with the dog. She'd have come, but I told her it was a thing I had to do on my own two feet." She turned the tape around in her fingers. "I didn't come to ask you for anything. I want to be very clear. I don't want an apology. There's nothing to apologize for. That's the trouble. For eleven years I've been trying to work out whom to blame, and there isn't anyone."

<i>No one to forgive,</i> thought Nora, and held very still.

"I stood on the stairs with my bag half packed," said Hannah, "and I told you I'd been offered a place, and I was waiting for you to say <i>stay.</i>"

"I know."

"I wasn't going to. I'd like that understood. I'd have said no. I'd have gone with my whole heart, I was twenty-three and the whole world was a door. But I wanted to be asked. I wanted to be a person somebody would ask." Her voice was perfectly level. She had had eleven years to practice. "And instead you gave me exactly what I'd said I wanted, so beautifully, so completely, that I couldn't even be angry. You said, <i>Of course you should go.</i> And I picked up my bag, and I thought, <i>she's let me go as though I weighed nothing.</i>"

Nora said nothing at all. She could feel the sentence turning over in her, the way a thing turns over in water. It had been the finest present she knew how to make. She had wrapped it with both hands. It had been the same sentence as <i>I don't need anything.</i> She had not seen it, in eleven years, because she had never once been on the receiving end.

She looked at the window, painted shut with love, and found that her mouth had opened.

"I wanted you to stay."

Hannah did not move.

"I've never said it. Not to you, not to anybody. I wanted you to stay so badly that I thought, if I said it, I'd become the person who held you back. I thought wanting was a kind of taking. I couldn't see how to want you and not have it cost you." Her voice was coming out in pieces, unsteady, and she let it. "So I gave you the other thing. I gave you the door. I'm so sorry. I don't mean that as an apology. I mean I'm sorry it took me this long to find out what the door was for."

There was a silence, and in it a clock somewhere in the house, ticking steadily on, as it must have done on the day Hannah was born.

"Thank you," said Hannah, quietly, and Nora heard that there was no door behind it, only a room. "It's eleven years late and it's perfect. Isn't that stupid?"

"No."

"Would you like to know what I'd have said?"

"No." A pause. "Yes."

"No," said Hannah, and they both began to laugh, helplessly, with their hands over their faces, on the narrow bed and the hard chair, in a room that smelled of dust and old paint and spring coming in around the edges of a window nobody had opened in forty years. "I'd have said no, Nora. And I'd have loved being asked."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They drank tea in the kitchen afterward, because it was something to do with their hands. Hannah talked about Ilse, and the dog, and a city where she had learned to be somebody who took up room. Nora listened, and for the first time in her life listened to a person she had loved without a single ounce of her attention going toward the next thing that might be needed.

"Is there someone?" said Hannah, at last.

"Her name is Sophie."

"And do you ask her for things?"

Nora considered this. "I asked her to hold one end of a window."

Hannah put down her mug. She laughed until she had to wipe her eyes on her sleeve, and Nora, to her own astonishment, laughed too. "Oh, Nora," Hannah said. "Oh, good. Was it heavy?"

"It was extremely heavy."

"And did she hold it?"

"She said, <i>Which end?</i>"

Hannah was quiet. "Keep that one," she said.

At the door, on the top step, with the whole length of Linden Street dropping away toward the river, she said, "Do you still have it? The lock off my window. I told myself you'd thrown it out. I've thought about it all week, and it's absurd."

"Third row," said Nora. "Fourth drawer from the left. At the back, in a handkerchief."

"Of course it is." Hannah looked at her with enormous tenderness. "You keep everything. It's the best thing about you, and I think it's very nearly the worst." She put one hand flat on Nora's arm, the way you lay a hand on a doorframe to see that it is steady. "Use it, Nora. A lock's for locking. Put it on something."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

She telephoned from the car at the foot of the hill, with the engine off, as she had promised. It rang once.

"I'm all right," said Nora.

"Tell me."

"Not on the telephone. May I come?" And, because she heard herself and it was true: "I'd like to."

"I'll leave the light on," said Sophie.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

But first she went down to the shed. It was almost dusk. The river was still high and running hard, and on the mole her father's boat hook lay where he had left it, beside the stack of drifted things, the cushion and the post and the oak frame, soft and silver in the failing light.

The sash from the schoolhouse had hung its week. The cords had settled, and it ran as smooth as a drawer. It needed only a lock.

She went to the keeping wall. Third row, fourth from the left. The brass lay in its dull heap, rubbed smooth by other people's thumbs, and under it, at the very back, her hand closed on the handkerchief. She unwrapped it for the second time since the autumn.

Some things were not spare parts, she had thought. That had been true. But there were two ways of not being a spare part, and she had only known one of them: to be kept, in the dark, in a drawer, for eleven years. The other was to be used.

She set it against the meeting rail and drove the screws. It was right at once, the way it had been right in a window on a hill. She turned the lever, and it closed with the small quiet click of a well-cut joint. It was a click she had learned to dread, and this time it was only a thing being held.

Then she turned it back, and pushed the sash up a hand's breadth, and stood with her palms on the sill.

The air came in smelling of thaw: wet earth, and cold stone, and something green that had not yet decided to be green. Somewhere upstream the ice was still going out, in pieces, in the dark, without any hurry at all.

She shut the shed door behind her. She left the window open.

###CHAPTER 8: Thank You for Letting Her Go

The cold frame rode to the Victorian house on the back seat of Sophie's car, wrapped in a gray moving blanket and belted in like a child.

"You're sure they'll want it," said Sophie, for the third time. She was driving with both hands on the wheel, which she did only when frightened.

"I'm not sure of anything. I made it."

"It's beautiful."

"It's a box with a lid." But she turned in her seat to check the blanket again, and Sophie, watching the road, smiled.

She had made it out of the river's oak. For three weeks, in the evenings, Nora had planed the drifted frame down through its silver to the pale, sweet-smelling wood beneath, cut the corners clean, hinged the lid, and glazed it with offcuts from the schoolhouse sash: twelve small panes of 1880s glass that bent the light a little when you moved your head. It was the kind of thing a gardener sets against a south wall in spring so that young plants can learn the weather before they have to meet it. She had never made one. She had stood at her bench with the oak under her hands and thought, <i>this is a thing that shelters others until they're ready to go out,</i> and had not allowed herself to think any further than that.

Her father had looked at it for some time, on the bench, before she wrapped it.

"Somebody's," he had said.

"Mine now."

He had grunted, which in her father was a paragraph.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

The house stood at the edge of town in its own deep garden, tall and gabled and slightly out of true, as though it had leaned in over the years to hear what was being said inside. Nora had never seen it. She had seen it described, in pieces, in the evenings: a glasshouse here, an oak there, a kitchen table with a burn in it the shape of Ireland. She had expected it to feel like a test. It felt like a place where somebody had put the kettle on.

Under the oak, in the green light of new leaves, stood a long table with a white cloth, and she counted the chairs without meaning to. Ten. Mara and Lena, three couples, Sophie, herself. She worked it out twice. One of them was hers. Nobody had said so, nobody had asked her to earn it. It had simply been set.

Three couples had come ahead of them and stood about in the grass with glasses, and she read them the only way she knew how, hands first: two women with their fingers laced like people who had stopped thinking about it; two with their shoulders touching; two who stood a careful inch apart and kept finding, with a sort of shy astonishment, that the inch had closed. They looked up, and smiled, and made room. That was all. Nobody required her to perform anything. It was the opposite of a test. It was like being handed a mug.

Mara came down the gravel to meet them. She was a woman in a canvas jacket with her sleeves pushed back, and Nora looked at her hands, because it was the only way she knew to meet a person, and Mara, who had very plainly been told, held them out, palms up, like someone offering a view.

Nora read them. Earth worked into every crease. A white scar across the left thumb. A wedding ring worn thin as a thread. A callus along the side of the first finger where a trowel had lived for thirty years. They were the hands of a person who had put a great many things in the ground, and then had the patience to wait.

"Sophie tells me you look at hands first," said Mara.

"I do."

"Good. It's where the truth is. The face has had too much practice."

Beside her stood Lena, with a dish towel over one shoulder, square and quiet and brown-armed, who took Nora's hand in a short firm grip, and held it a half-second longer than the greeting required. <i>A person who has carried a great many chairs,</i> thought Nora, and was moved, absurdly, to be so exactly right.

"I brought something," she said. She had practiced the sentence in the car, and it came out nothing like the practice. "It isn't for the house. It's for the garden. If you don't want it, I'll take it home, it won't—"

Sophie had already lifted the blanket.

Mara stopped talking. She walked all the way around the cold frame, once, slowly, as one walks round a horse. She laid her palm flat on the oak. She ran her thumb down the hinge, and along the edge of the old glass, and watched her own face bend in it.

"Where did the wood come from?"

"The river. It came down in March, in the thaw. Somebody's window. I never found out whose."

"Then it's been somewhere," said Mara, "and now it's here."

She did not say <i>you shouldn't have.</i> She did not say <i>it's too much.</i> She bent, and took the cold frame into her arms, refusing Sophie's offered hand, and carried it herself across the grass to the glasshouse door, where she set it down in the sun against the glass wall and stood back with her hands on her hips.

"Tomatoes," she said, to nobody. "The first ones. Tomorrow."

That was all. It was thanks, and it was complete. Nora stood watching with a feeling she could not name at first, and understood only on the drive home. It was envy: not of what Mara had, but of how easily she had it. She had received the thing the way a field receives rain, with no ledger, no <i>I'll make it up to you,</i> simply giving it somewhere to go.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They ate under the tree as the afternoon light softened. The conversation was easy and went in circles: fragments of earlier crossings, mentions of fear and courage, the strange persistent way love had kept arriving once the door was open. Nora said little. She had not known that a table could be so full of people who needed nothing from her.

Sophie spoke late, and her voice was steady.

"I used to think something was wrong with me," she said. "That I was missing the part that lets a person stay. It turned out I was only missing the right conditions, and the right examples." She looked at Mara and Lena with an expression of unguarded love. "You gave me both. And then you let me leave when it was time."

Nora laid her hand flat on Sophie's back, between the shoulder blades, and felt the breath go in and out of her. She felt the last sentence go through her too, on its way. <i>And then you let me leave.</i> She looked across the table and saw Mara's eyes fill and not be stopped, and saw Lena's fingers find Mara's under the cloth and close. She looked at them the way she would look at two people carrying a heavy sash between them: nothing to see, nothing at all, but the steadiness.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

As evening came the gathering loosened into smaller knots, and Nora slipped away, as she did, to the edge of things.

The cold frame stood where Mara had put it, glowing faintly in the low sun. Someone, unable to wait until tomorrow, had already set a flat of seedlings in it, a dozen small green heads pushing up against the old glass as though they wanted to see what it did to the light. Nora stood and looked at them for a long time.

"She lasted until four o'clock," said Lena, behind her. "I'd given her until six."

She had two mugs. She held one out, and said, "How do you take it?"

"As it comes."

"Good answer." Lena stood beside her and looked at the seedlings. "She has not stopped looking at it all afternoon. I thought you should know. Some people give things and never find out what became of them."

Nora held the mug in both hands.

"I wanted to say something to you," she said. "I don't have a way of saying it that isn't a speech."

"Then say it short."

"Thank you for letting her go."

Lena did not move. Steam rose between them and was lost.

"I know it cost something. I don't know how much. I wanted you to hear it from the other side." She stopped, because there was no more. It was a short speech, and it was done.

For a moment she thought she had been wrong to say it. Then Lena turned her head, and her face was open in a way Nora had not yet seen it, and she said, quietly, "Thank you for being the one she went to."

The old reflex rose at once in Nora, ready-made, worn smooth: <i>it was nothing, I only did what anyone would.</i> She felt it come up like a hand to a hot stove. And, for once, she left it where it was.

"I'm glad it was me," she said.

It was surprisingly easy once it was out.

"That's the right answer," said Lena. "Most people say it was nothing." She turned the mug in her hands. "It cost. Every day of it. I'd do it again tomorrow. I want that said, so you don't think I'm being noble. I'm not. I'm only the person who happened to be standing there."

"How do you do it?" Nora heard her own voice go lower. "Give someone all of it, and then let go, and not have it turn into something you've been robbed of?"

"You say what it is at the start. Out loud, in the first week, before anyone's attached. <i>This is a bridge.</i> It sounds cold." Lena shrugged. "It's the warmest thing I know."

"Sophie told me. She said it to me in November." <i>In the dark. With me already open.</i>

"I know. She came the next morning, white as paper, and said she'd told you everything. She cried for an hour. Mara made tea." Lena's eyes were steady. "She said you said <i>thank you.</i> She said it was the kindest thing anyone had ever said to her, and she didn't know what to do with it."

Nora said nothing. She looked at the seedlings.

"Do you know what a bridge wants, at the far end?"

"A bank."

"A landing. Somebody on the other side who isn't going anywhere." Lena smiled. "That's you."

"I've been told I stay where the crossing is." It came out dry, and Lena laughed, the first full laugh Nora had heard from her. "My father's a ferryman. I grew up at a landing."

"Then you know both halves." Lena set her mug on the glasshouse sill. "We carry someone across, and we don't get to go with them."

"No."

"And it doesn't get easier," said Lena. "It gets more familiar. That's all I have. It isn't much."

"It's a lot," said Nora, and meant it.

They stood in the quiet. A blackbird was going over its evening in the hedge. Far off under the oak, someone laughed, and someone else began to clear the plates.

"May I say one more thing?" said Lena. "It isn't advice."

"Yes."

"She would like to be asked for things. I don't mean the window. She told us about the window, she brought it to us like a child with a feather." Her mouth moved. "I mean the other kind. The kind that would cost you something to say. She would like, very much, to be asked to stay."

Nora's hands were still around the mug.

"I said that to someone, recently," she said slowly. "A long time late. I haven't said it to her."

"I know. She'll wait. She's good at it now." Lena picked up her mug. "I only say it because I think someone should say it to the one who stays where the crossing is. It's allowed."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They drove home in the long blue dusk with the empty blanket folded on the back seat. Sophie was quiet for a mile, and then she said, "What did Lena say to you?"

"I said thank you."

"For what?"

"For letting you go."

Sophie's hands moved on the wheel. She said nothing, and kept saying it for another half mile, in the way of a person who has been handed something too large to put down.

"You're very strange," she said finally, her voice wet.

"I'm told."

They passed the turning for the church road, and the long straight under the poplars, and the first lights of the town. Nora watched them come. She was composing a sentence, and she had never composed one before for this purpose, and it was taking a long time.

"Sophie."

"Mm."

"I'd like you to stay tonight."

Sophie glanced across, startled. "I always do."

"I know. You always do. I've never asked."

There was a long silence. A truck went by, and its lights slid over both their faces.

"Ask me again," said Sophie, very low.

"Will you stay?"

"Yes."

She said it so quietly that Nora had to lean to hear it. It was not the yes of a person who has been invited. It was the yes of a person who has been <i>asked,</i> and the difference, Nora saw, was the whole of the matter.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

There were twenty-two steps up from the street. Nora began to count them, as she always did, and was half-way through the second flight when she heard a second voice, a half-step behind her own, under its breath, like someone learning a hymn.

<i>Seventeen. Eighteen.</i>

She did not stop. She did not look round. She went on up, and counted to the top, and they arrived together.

###CHAPTER 9: Six O'Clock

Nora had a rule about floods, which was that you did not wait for them to arrive.

The river had been rising since Sunday. On Monday her father had read it off the doorframe in the flat voice he kept for numbers, and on Tuesday he had stopped reading it and only stood on the mole with his hands behind him, which was worse. By Wednesday morning the water was over the second stone and walking toward the third, brown and thick as cocoa, carrying whole trees. The spring melt had come down from the hills all at once, the way it did perhaps one year in seven. The shed stood on the low ground. It had stood there for a hundred and ninety years and been under twice.

She began at dawn.

The sandbags came first. Seventy-one of them, from the stack under the eaves, carried two at a time to the doorway and laid in a staggered wall, three high, with the seams broken like brickwork. Then the tools: the lathe up on its blocks, the planes and the chisels up to the loft in their wooden trays, the glass in its crates, every sash that was not hanging in a frame. The loft was nine stairs up. She counted them each trip, and each trip the number sounded a little more like a sentence.

Her father came down at nine and stood in the doorway.

"Do you want—" he began.

"I've got it."

He looked at the sandbags. He looked at her. Then he looked, for a long moment, at his own hands, which opened and closed once at his sides, like a man testing whether a thing he had always owned was still there.

"Good," he said, and went back up the path.

It was the old sentence. She heard it leave her mouth, smooth as a river stone, and she knew exactly what it cost and who paid, and she said it anyway, because there was a flood in the doorway and it was easier. That was the secret of the old sentence: it was always easier to pick up than to set down.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

At noon the telephone buzzed in her apron.

<i>How's the river?</i> wrote Sophie.

<i>Fine. Busy.</i>

A minute later: <i>I could bring supper at six. Say stop and I'll stay home.</i>

Nora stood with the phone in her hand and a sandbag at her feet. Her thumb moved. She typed, <i>Yes, please, come,</i> and looked at it, and deleted it.

She had asked a handful of times since December, for a window, for a night, and each time it had been wonderful, and the wonder was still on her like a coat. But asking was a thing that ran out. It was like money, she had always believed: you were allowed a certain amount, and after that you were taking. She put the telephone away without answering. It did not occur to her that this was an answer.

At four the kitchen window above the shed opened, a minute before the ring. She worked on beneath it, under the long low music of her father's voice coming down through the boards like water through sand, and tried not to hear that it was shorter than usual. Eleven minutes. When it stopped she heard him stand, for a time, in the middle of the floor.

He did not come down. She was grateful, and ashamed, and could not have said which was larger.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

By half past five the lower half of the keeping wall was bare.

It had taken two hours. Everything below her knee came out, drawer by drawer, and went up the nine stairs in her arms: the chemist's tiny ones, the chandler's, the dozen that had held somebody's linens. The empty spaces stood open along the wall like the gaps in a smile. The water, meanwhile, had reached the third stone. It lay along the front of the sandbags with a flat, shining patience, not hurrying, not needing to. On the doorframe the pencil marks stood dry and small and neat in three hands: hers, her father's, and, four inches above the highest of the others, her grandfather's, faded and thick, drawn in a year nobody remembered. She was not looking at them. She was looking at the floor.

The blue drawer was the last.

She had left it on purpose. She knew that. She slid it out of its place, at the height of her knee, and it was lighter than she remembered. The glazing points rattled. The tacks. A chipped blue enamel button slid from one end to the other, with a small clear sound.

She started up the stairs with it. One. Two. Three. She had carried this drawer out of a kitchen six years ago, and since then she had set it in the wall like a stone in a field and walked around it. She had never carried it up anywhere. Four. Five.

On the fifth stair she stopped, because she could not go on.

It was not dramatic. Her legs did not give way. She simply found, with the odd calm of a person watching someone else's hands, that the drawer was in her lap and she was sitting on the stairs, and that she did not know how to stand. The count had come apart in her. Six, she thought, seven, and the numbers would not hold. She was so tired, she understood, that it had a sound: a high thin note, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass.

She looked at the button. And something in her, which had been holding since five in the morning, since Sunday, since a good deal earlier, quietly let go.

There was no noise. That was the strangest thing. The tears came down her face and onto the chipped blue enamel, and she watched them fall, and she did not lift a hand to stop them, because both hands were holding the drawer.

Across the water, the church clock began to strike six.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

She heard the car on the gravel at the third stroke. She heard the door of it, and the quick light steps, and the shed door scrape on the sandbags. At the fifth stroke she heard the steps stop.

At the sixth, nobody said anything at all.

Sophie stood in the shed doorway, with a paper sack in each arm and the long gold light of the evening behind her, and took it in: the bare wall, the sandbags, the water at the doorstep. And, up the stairs in the half dark, the woman with a drawer in her lap who had not looked round.

Nora felt her see it. She felt the whole of it arrive.

She waited for the questions. <i>Are you all right. What happened. What do you need.</i> They were the questions that came, and she had no answer to any of them, and she braced for the sound of them like a person bracing for a step that was not there.

They did not come.

Sophie set down the sacks, very softly, on the bench. She crossed the floor. She did not come up the stairs. She sat down on the bottom step, with her back to Nora, her shoulders a little square, as someone sits who has decided to wait for a train. She did not turn her head.

"I'll wait," she said to the room.

That was all.

And Nora, on the fifth stair, with her face wet and her mouth open, understood that she was being given something she had given a hundred times and never once received: someone's back, turned to her, in order that she might be unobserved. It was a kind of door. It was the kind she had held for others, standing at the end of a corridor, looking at the wall.

She cried then, truly. Not hard, not loud. It took a long while. The light went from gold to rose to gray along the edge of the open door, and the water slapped softly at the sandbags, and neither of them said a word, and the clock across the river struck the half hour and then, after a long time, seven.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"You didn't say stop," said Sophie, to the doorway, when it had been quiet for a time.

"I didn't say anything."

"I know. I thought that might be a kind of yes." She still did not turn. "I took the chance. I'd have gone home if I'd been wrong."

"I wanted you to come." Her voice was cracked and small. "I wrote it, and I deleted it."

"Say it now."

"I wanted you to come. I couldn't—" She stopped. "I thought it would run out."

"What would?"

"Asking."

Sophie turned then, finally, on the step, and her face in the failing light was wet too. "There's no quota, Nora," she said. "There was never a quota. I think you were told there was, once, by someone who was trying to be kind."

Nora looked at her for a long time. She thought of Tessa, and of Maren's hand on her cheek. <i>Stop paying me.</i> She thought that she had spent a great part of her life settling a debt nobody had run up.

"I can't pay it back," she said.

"I know." Sophie's mouth shook. "I'd be offended if you tried."

It was so exactly the right thing that Nora laughed, a ragged, hiccuping, hopeless laugh, and then was crying again, and Sophie was up the stairs and on the step below her, not touching, only there, with her hands open on her knees.

"Can I carry something?" said Sophie.

Nora looked down at the drawer.

She had never let anyone carry it. She had never let anyone touch it, not Ines, not her father, not even once. It was the heaviest thing in the shed. She held it a moment longer, in both arms, as one holds something one is about to put into the water.

Then she held it out.

Sophie took it as if it were full of something that could spill. She did not look at the button, or the tacks, or the faded blue. She carried it up the last four stairs, and set it on the loft shelf beside the others, square and level, with the care of a person who has been given a thing in trust and does not need to be told what it is. She came down again. She asked nothing about it. She never would.

Nora sat on the stair, and put her hands in her lap, and found that they were empty and that nothing terrible had happened.

<i>That's what it is,</i> she thought. <i>It isn't a debt. It's a place for something to go.</i>

She thought of Mara's arms, under the oak, around the cold frame. <i>The way a field receives rain.</i> It hurt, a little. She had not expected that. It hurt like a limb coming back after being slept on, the blood returning in needles. She would not have given it up for anything.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They ate supper on the bench, out of the paper sacks, by the light of one bulb. Bread and a hard cheese and olives and an apple each, and a bottle of something cold and pale from the market. Sophie had brought two forks, and then, from the bottom of the sack, a third, and looked at it a little guiltily, and put it away.

At nine the water stopped.

It stopped at the third sandbag, a hand's breadth below the top, and it stood there. They both saw it happen. The brown slide along the doorstep slowed, and thickened, and lay still, and for the first time in four days there was no sound of it climbing. Out past the mole the river went on being enormous, but it had given up on the shed.

"It's turned," said Nora.

"How do you know?"

"I just do."

A shadow fell across the doorway. Her father stood on the path with a thermos, a pair of blankets over one arm, and his hat in his hand, which she had not seen in years. He looked at the water. He looked at the wall of sandbags, and at the bare gaps in the keeping wall, and then, for a good long while, at the two of them sitting on the bench in a pool of bulb light with their shoulders touching.

"She's stopped," he said to the river. "Seven inches under the line."

"I know," said Nora.

"Good." He held out the thermos, to Sophie, not to her. Sophie took it. "The lane's under at the poplars," he said. "You won't get out tonight. The church road's flooded too, at the bridge."

"Oh," said Sophie, rather pink. "Then I'll sleep in the car."

"There's a bed in the back room. It's made up."

Nora looked at him.

It was, she thought, very odd. Nobody had slept in the back room in years. The bed in it had not been made, as far as she knew, since the last time her mother stayed. She noticed this the way she noticed everything, from the corner of the eye. She put it where she put such things, in that long drawer of the unasked, and found, to her surprise, that the drawer had stopped closing. It was too full. Something was sticking out of it, a corner, a strap, the tail of a thing.

She did not look at it. Not tonight. There would be a night for that, too.

"Thank you," she said.

Her father nodded. He put his hat on, and took it off again, and stood a moment longer than the matter required, with his eyes on his daughter's face. "You'll be tired in the morning," he said.

"I expect so."

"Good," he said, and meant, she knew, something very different, and went back up the path with the lamp-light from the kitchen window moving along the wet grass ahead of him.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Before they went up, Sophie stopped at the doorframe.

The pencil marks climbed it in their neat columns, hers and her father's, small and steady, and above them the line of the grandfather, faded and thick. Sophie stood looking at the last one, which was Nora's, from that morning, written in a hand so tired that it leaned.

"May I?" said Sophie.

"May you what?"

"Put one. For tonight. The water, where it stopped." She held out her hand. "Only if it's yours to give."

It was a long moment.

<i>Three hands: hers, her father's, her grandfather's.</i> The shed had never held a fourth. It was an old doorframe, and a private one, and it had kept the river's count for a century without any help from a stranger.

Nora put the pencil in her hand.

Sophie wrote it with her tongue between her teeth, concentrating. A line, level, at the place where the water had turned. <i>Wed.</i> And a small neat number. Her hand was rounder than any of the others on that wood, and a little slanted, and very clear. It looked, among them, like a person who intended to stay.

Nora counted the steps up to the house. She did not say them aloud.

Sophie counted with her.

###CHAPTER 10: Still Here

Six years of Christmas cards lived in a shoebox on the loft shelf, under a coil of old sash cord, and Nora had never once thought of them as a correspondence. She had thought of them as weather.

They came every December, in the first week, in a plain envelope with a round, unhurried hand. Inside was a card Maren had made herself, with a small drawing in colored pencil and underneath it, always, the same two words. A kettle. A robin on a fence. Two mugs, and a name Nora did not know. A pram. A blue handprint, small enough to be covered by a coin. And last December, in crayon, a boat with a very large sail, and across the bottom, in a different hand, in capitals, <i>LOTTE SAYS BOAT.</i>

<i>Still here. M.</i>

Nora had read every one of them standing up. She had put every one in the box. She had never answered, not out of coldness, but because to answer a thing was to admit it had been a question, and then it might ask for something back.

The box sat now on the loft shelf at the end of a row of drawers. At the other end, square and level, where a woman had put it down in the dusk as if it were full of something that could spill, sat the blue one. Nora had been walking past the two of them for six weeks before she saw that they were looking at each other.

She wrote the card on a Tuesday, at the bench, on the back of a glazing ledger page, three times. The third time she did not tear it up.

<i>Maren,</i>

<i>Six cards. I read every one, standing, which you'll know. I never answered. I'm sorry.</i>

<i>The shed flooded in April. I'm all right. Would you come?</i>

<i>N.</i>

She looked at it for a long time. Then, because it was true, she wrote underneath, very small:

<i>P.S. Still here.</i>

The reply was a postcard of a lighthouse, and it came in four days, which was fast enough to be alarming.

<i>Saturday the 14th. Ruth says GO. May I bring them? They will stay on the bank. Lotte wants to see a real boat. M.</i>

<i>May I.</i> Nora held it in the sun by the door. She had asked, and been asked. She stood there until the heron on the far bank moved, and then she laughed, once, out loud, to nobody, a short surprised sound like a drawer sliding.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"Maren is coming tomorrow," she said that night.

They were sitting in the apartment's one good window, with the sash pushed up and the street going quiet below. Sophie had her feet up on the sill and a tub of ice cream balanced on her knees, and she did not stop eating, which Nora took, correctly, as tact.

"Do you want me far away," said Sophie, "or near?"

It was the question Nora had been dreading and hoping for, in about equal parts. She turned it over, honestly, and for once found there was an answer at the bottom.

"Near," she said. "In the shed. Doing the chisel." She heard her voice go odd. "I want to talk to her alone. But I want to know you're in the next room."

Sophie put the spoon down.

"I can do that," she said, quietly. "I can be extremely in the next room."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

June had put the river down to its summer self, clear and low and warm, so that you could see the stones on the bottom, each with its own green beard moving in the current. The ferry was in the water for the first time since September. She rode at her mooring beside the mole, new tar black along the seams, and the light came off the river and went up her side in long moving ribbons. Dragonflies worked the reeds. The elder along the lane was in flower and the whole landing smelled of it, sweet and a little sour, like a child's cough medicine.

Sophie was at the bench with a chisel and a stone. She could do it now, honing the edge with her eyes half shut, her tongue between her teeth, the thin sound of steel on oilstone as steady as breathing. She did not look up when the car came down the lane. She only said, to the stone, "I'm right here," and went on.

Nora stood in the doorway and watched it arrive.

It was a small blue car, and it stopped where the lane ran out, by the ferry cradle. Two people got out of the front and one of the back, a very small one in a yellow rain hat, in the sunshine, who went straight for the water and had to be caught. The tall woman who caught her was in her forties, browned from the sun, with her hands in the back pockets of her jeans between whiles: an ambulance driver's calm, thought Nora, with no idea how she knew. She lifted the child onto one hip and looked across the grass at the shed, and nodded once, briefly, to Nora, as to a colleague.

The third was Maren.

She was broader than she'd been, and softer at the edges, the way people get when they have been laughed at for years by someone very small. Her hair had gone short and gray-streaked. She was wearing a blue dress with pockets, and her hands, which Nora read before she read anything else, had a child's plaster across one forefinger and a smudge of poster paint on the heel of the right. It was the same hand. It was entirely different.

<i>She looks,</i> thought Nora, <i>like someone who has been fed regularly and told the truth.</i>

"Hello, Nora," said Maren.

"Hello."

Neither of them moved. Then Maren said, "You wrote."

"I answered."

"Six years late."

"Seven, if you count the first."

Maren laughed, and the laugh was exactly the same, low and helpless and a little cracked at the edges, and the whole shed behind Nora seemed to hold its breath and let it out.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

At the head of the mole, under the elder, stood the bench the ferrymen had always used, a long plank on two stumps, worn so smooth by years of waiting that it shone like the inside of a shell. They sat on it with a foot between them. On the bank, Ruth had spread a blanket, and the child was building something out of river stones with great seriousness and no help. A little farther along the shore her father, who had come down at the sound of the car, was standing with the yellow hat in his hand, having apparently been given it.

"I'd like to say something," said Maren. "It's going to take a minute. I've practiced it in the car for three hours, and Ruth has heard it eleven times, and it's still bad."

"Say it badly."

"That last night. The kitchen." Maren's eyes were on the water. "I said it was lonely, loving someone who needs nothing. I was right about the loneliness. I was wrong about the grammar."

Nora held very still.

"You weren't a person who needed nothing. You were a person who had decided that needing was a form of taking. I knew that. I watched you do it for five years, with the hinges and the kettle and the radiators, mending everything in the house so that nothing would ever have to be asked of anybody. And I thought: if I ask for something big enough, she'll have to." A short breath. "I wanted a child, Nora. For three years I wanted it, and I never said so. Because I knew what you'd say. You'd have said, <i>Of course, if you want.</i> And I didn't want <i>of course.</i> I wanted you to <i>want.</i>"

The elder dropped a few small flowers on the plank between them.

"You were waiting for me to ask. I was waiting for you to want. And we sat across that table being exquisitely kind to each other, until the fire went out." Maren turned and looked at her at last. "I put all of it on you, and I called it a diagnosis. It wasn't a diagnosis, Nora. It was half mine."

Nora had a sensation she could only compare to the moment a sash comes free of the cords after years of binding: no sound, but the whole frame suddenly lighter than a thing that size had any right to be. She had carried that sentence for six years. <i>To love someone who needs nothing.</i> She had worn it the way you wear a coat you have forgotten is not your own. She had never once looked at the lining and seen that someone else's name was sewn in it.

"I would have wanted it," she said, slowly. "I think. If you'd said. I just didn't know it was allowed."

"Neither did I," said Maren.

They sat there. A dragonfly stitched the air over the water, and came back, and left.

"Are you happy?" Nora said at last. It was a foolish question, with the answer standing thirty yards off, handing a stone to a three-year-old. She asked it anyway, because it was the sort of thing a person said when she wanted to be told something she already knew.

"Look," said Maren, and did not point.

Nora looked. The tall woman had knelt by the blanket and was being shown, with enormous gravity, a very small gray stone. The child's face was lit from inside like a window. It was a simple thing, as plain as bread, and Nora felt it enter her without any envy at all. It was only that it was good, and she was glad, and the gladness had no hook in it.

"She's wonderful," she said.

"She's <i>a lot,</i>" said Maren. "Come to dinner in August. Lotte wants to show you her boat."

<i>Of course,</i> Nora began to say. It was already in her mouth, smooth as a river stone, the oldest sentence she owned. She stopped it with her teeth.

"I'd like that," she said.

It sounded wrong. She tried it again.

"I want to."

And Maren's face did something Nora would not forget: it opened all the way down, like a window thrown wide on a day when someone has been waiting a long time for the weather to change.

"There it is," she said.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Afterward, Nora took her up to the shed, because there was something she wanted her to see.

Sophie was still at the bench, with the chisel across her knee and an edge on it that would have shaved a hair, and she stood up as they came in, wiping her hands on her trousers, which she did not usually do. She looked, Nora saw, a little pale.

"Sophie," Nora said. "This is Maren."

They shook hands. Maren held on a moment, as Lena had, as people do who read what they are holding. She looked at Sophie's hands, and then, with a flick, at Nora's, and Nora knew in that instant that something had been decided that she had not been consulted on.

"You carried the drawer," said Maren.

Sophie flushed to the roots of her hair. "She told you?"

"Nobody told me." Maren looked up at the loft. "I came in the door and saw it on the shelf."

They climbed the nine stairs. The blue drawer stood where Sophie had set it, beside the shoebox, faded at the edges to the color of a tired sky. Maren stood in front of it for some time with her arms at her sides. She did not touch it. When she did, it was with two fingertips on the painted front, the way you would touch the forehead of a sleeping child.

"It looks cheerful," she said.

"It does," said Nora.

They both laughed, helplessly, and at once, and Sophie, standing three steps down in the half-dark, laughed too, without the faintest idea why, because it was a very good sound.

"Do you know she counts?" said Maren, to Sophie, wiping her eyes.

"Twenty-two," said Sophie at once.

Maren stopped. She looked at Sophie. She looked at Nora, who was studying the rafters with great concentration. And she said, very softly, "Oh. You count with her."

"A half-step behind," said Sophie. "She doesn't mind."

"No," said Maren. "I don't suppose she does." She took Sophie's hand and held it in both of hers for a moment, and Nora understood that she was being handed over. It was not a transfer of property. It was a thing more like a key, pressed into a palm by someone who had held it a good while and was glad to see it go to a door that fit.

"She's very good with hinges," said Maren.

"I know," said Sophie. "I've seen them."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They drove away at four, in the long yellow light, with a small hand waving from the back window until the lane bent. Her father had gone up the path again. On the bank, flattened in the grass, lay a smooth gray stone the size of an egg, placed on the end of the mooring post where a child could find it.

Sophie came and stood beside Nora at the end of the mole, a little apart, not touching, with the water going by beneath them in long clear plaits.

"She wanted a child," said Nora. "And I never knew."

"Yes."

"I don't know if I do." It was out before she had decided to say it. "I've never asked myself. I've never asked myself anything, I don't think. I've been so occupied with what other people would want, and whether I was allowed to give it." She looked at the ferry, riding easy at her mooring. "I thought wanting was a kind of taking. I thought so for thirty years."

"And now?"

Nora thought of a child's face lit like a window, and a plaster on a forefinger, and a long tall woman kneeling in the grass. She thought of a thin sound of steel on stone, steady as breathing, coming from the next room.

<i>I want,</i> she thought, and let the word stand, as one lets a stranger in at the door and is surprised to find he has your mother's walk. <i>I want something with a boat on a bank and a person on it. I want it with her.</i>

She did not say it. She was not ready. There was a way to say it that would be a real ask, and she had not found it yet, and she knew that when she did it would cost more than a window, and more than a night, and that it would be worth every penny. She only knew, for the first time, that there was something to ask for.

"I think," said Nora, "I'd like to find out."

Sophie did not reach for her. She did not say <i>with me.</i> She stood very still at the end of the mole, with the sun on her hair and the river in her eyes, and said, in a voice that was only slightly steady, "I'm not in any hurry."

"I know," said Nora.

"Good," said Sophie, and laughed, wetly, because it was Nora's father's word, and she had caught it from the air.

They stood and watched the ferry. Behind them, in the shed, the light climbed the nine-foot window and made the river bend.

###CHAPTER 11: The Father's Boat

Her father waited for the Thursday of the first hot week in July, and then he came down to the shed at ten in the morning in his Sunday shirt.

Nora saw it from the bench. It was the shirt that told her, before she had seen his face: white, a little yellow along the old folds, buttoned to the neck. He stopped in the doorway with his hat in one hand. She looked at his hands first, as she always did, and found that the hat brim was gripped so hard the felt had gone pale under his thumbs.

"Do you want—" he began.

<i>I've got it,</i> said her mouth, or nearly. It was always ready, that sentence. It lay just behind her teeth like a coin in a pocket.

But he had stopped. He stood with the end of the question hanging in the dusty air between them, and she saw that his hands were not offering. They were shaking, very slightly, the way a boat shakes when the wire takes up.

"No," he said. "That's the wrong way round."

He drew a breath like a man about to go into cold water.

"I would like you to row me across. This afternoon, if you can spare the time." And then, with a great effort, as if the next part weighed more than the first: "I would like it to be you."

<i>Of course,</i> rose in her, smooth as a river stone, the oldest sentence she owned, and she felt it come all the way up to her tongue before she understood what was underneath it. It was a plain thing, and so large she could barely lift it. She was being asked. For forty years he had carried her over everything, and he had never once, not for a cup of tea, not for a screw, put a hand out and said <i>this is for me.</i>

She set down the chisel.

"Yes," she said. Then, because that was only half, "I want to."

Her father nodded, slowly. The hat turned a quarter-turn in his hands.

"Which end?" said Nora, because her voice needed something to do.

He looked at her. Then something moved at the corner of his mouth, and the thing that came out of him was a short, low, startled sound, helpless and a little cracked at the edges, which she had heard perhaps four times in her life face to face.

"The one with the oars," he said.

It was the fifth.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"He asked me for something," she told Sophie at noon.

Sophie had come with lunch in a basket and a hat she did not need, and she was sitting on the end of the bench with a peach in her hand, which she stopped holding as if it had gone out of her mind. For one moment her face did something Nora could not read at all. It was not surprise. It was more like watching a person set down a heavy bag at the end of a very long walk, and not yet trust that it was allowed.

"What did he ask?"

"To be rowed across. To the churchyard." Nora heard how it sounded. "He hasn't been since they buried my grandfather. It's four miles round by the bridge and forty yards by water, and he's never made himself do the four, and he couldn't ask anyone for the forty." She turned a drill bit over in her fingers. "I said yes."

"Of course you did."

"I said <i>I want to.</i>"

Sophie did look at her then. "And do you?"

Nora had to think. It was a strange thing, to have to think. She found that the answer was there at the bottom, like a stone in clear water, with a green beard moving on it.

"Yes," she said. "I think it's the first thing he's ever given me." She frowned. "Isn't that a mad way to put it? He's asked for something. That's the gift."

"No," said Sophie, rather unsteadily, and bit the peach. "I don't think that's mad at all."

There was something in the next breath, a pause that went on a fraction too long, and Nora, who read hands first, saw that the one holding the peach had gone a little white at the knuckle, as if it were holding itself still on purpose. She noticed it, and gave it a quiet room in that long drawer of hers, the one that would not close. It had stopped being a drawer, she thought. It had become a sort of cellar.

"I'll be here," said Sophie, "on this bench. Extremely on the bench."

"I know."

"Take as long as you need." A beat. "Take longer, if it goes well."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

The skiff lay under the willow at the downstream end of the landing, the way it had lain every summer of Nora's life. It was an old clinker boat, gray-green, no longer than a tall man laid flat three times, with no name, because her grandfather had said a boat that knew its own name would get ideas. Her father did not use it much. It was the one he had rowed <i>her</i> in, as a child, to the far bank and back, on days when the ferry was out and a girl needed to see what the crossing was for.

He stood on the stones in his white shirt, with his hat on, and did not seem to know what to do.

Nora understood this at once. He had held the boat for ten thousand people. He had handed every one of them in: elbow, wrist, a steadying hand, <i>mind the thwart.</i> He had never in his life been the one who stepped. She stood in the shallows, with her sandals in her hand and the water going over her ankles, and held the gunwale out toward him, and said nothing at all.

He took the weight on his hand, stepped over, and sat down in the stern with his hands in his lap like a man in a doctor's waiting room.

She pushed off.

The river took them in its long warm green arms, and for a time there was only the sound of oars: the soft knock in the locks, the drip off the blades, the fine continuous whisper of the hull. She rowed well. She had learned on this boat with her father's hand over hers. Out here the river was wide and slow and the color of a bottle held to the light, and the church tower stood over the far bank among its trees, and a kingfisher went across them both like a thrown needle.

Her father sat facing her. She had never in her life seen him this still. He kept his eyes on the church, then on the water, then, once, briefly, on the oars, as if checking that they were doing what he'd have done.

"Your mother says," he said, to the middle of the river, "that I have never asked you for anything."

Nora missed a stroke. The boat gave a small, offended wobble.

"That was Wednesday," he went on, in the flat voice he kept for numbers. "The call. I told her about the morning of the flood. <i>Do you want</i>, and <i>I've got it.</i> I thought she'd say it was nothing. She was quiet for some time." He looked at his hands. "Then she said, <i>You didn't ask her. You offered. Those are different.</i> She said offering lets you say no. She said I'd been offering you things for thirty years so that you would never have to refuse me, and so that I would never have to be refused."

The oars dipped and rose. Nora said nothing. She did not trust it.

"She said, <i>Ask her for something. Something that's for you.</i> And she hung up."

"That's why it was eleven minutes."

"That's why it was eleven minutes." His mouth did the thing again, a flicker. "She said I'd have to practice on somebody else. She said she wouldn't have it spent on her."

And Nora saw it then, plainly, all at once, with the oars dripping and a heron lifting off the far shallows like a sheet shaken out of a window. Her mother, in her apartment in town, twelve miles of wire away, who laughed too loudly at funerals, who had known him forty years and could finish every sentence of his, and who had <i>not</i> finished this one. Who had put down the telephone, so that he would have to say the words to the person they were for.

<i>She gave them to him,</i> thought Nora, <i>and she took no credit. She went and stood in the kitchen with her hands in the sink.</i> It was a sort of love she had not had a name for. It was like the way you leave the latch off a door.

"So," said her father. "It's practice."

"It's not practice," said Nora, rather hoarsely. "You asked. It isn't practice if it works."

He looked at her, then. Properly. It went on for a very long time.

"I've never been rowed," he said. "Did you know that? I have crossed this water more times than there are boards in that shed, and I have never once been the one who sat down."

"I know," said Nora. "I could tell by the way you got in."

The helpless sound again. She caught it, and kept it, and rowed on.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

The far landing was a flight of six stone steps under an alder, green with old wet. She shipped the oars and let the skiff come in on her own, and held it, and this time her father did not wait to be handed. He stood, and found his balance, and reached out and took her wrist. It was the first time he had touched her since she was twelve. His hand was dry and very warm and not quite steady.

"Thank you," he said, to the wrist.

"You haven't gotten out yet."

"I'm saying it early," said her father, "in case I don't manage later."

The path went up through the long grass, past a hedge of dog roses, to a lych-gate with moss on its tiles. The church was a plain gray thing with a squat tower. The churchyard lay behind it on the slope toward the water, and it was full of the slow summer sound of bees. She had never been inside it. It had not occurred to her, until her father opened the gate, how odd that was: that all her life she had sat forty yards from this place, and the whole width of a river had been enough to make it somewhere else.

The stone was near the bottom, on the river side, under a yew. It was small and plain and gray, and it had an oar cut into the top of it, and below the oar the name, which was her own, and two dates. She had a grandfather. It was an arithmetic fact. It had never been anything else until she stood in front of the dates.

Her father took off his hat.

He did not kneel or speak for some time. Then he said, in the flat voice, in the voice for numbers, to the stone:

"She's running low. Clear. Four weeks of no rain." A pause. "Nora's got the landing."

Nora stood very still.

"She's had a flood, and got through it. The shed has a new floor." Another pause, and the hat turned once. "She's got someone, Father. I wanted you to know."

A bee went across the gap between them and was gone. Nora did not look at him. She understood that she was being allowed not to, the way one is allowed not to look at someone undressing a wound. She kept her eyes on the oar cut in the gray stone, and found that it had gone soft at the edges.

After a minute he put his hat on and said in his usual voice, "That's done, then."

"Is it?"

"I think so." He looked at the grass. "I'd been afraid it would take longer."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

There was a bench under the yew, a long plank on two stone legs, worn pale by the weather. Her father sat down on the left end of it, close to the arm, and left the rest of the seat empty, as a man does who is used to being joined. He did not seem to know that he had done it.

Nora sat down at the other end. In the weathered wood between them there were two small rings, side by side, each the width of a cup, and a little darker than the grain.

She looked at them. She looked at the church, at the river, at nothing.

<i>It's a bench,</i> she thought. <i>People sit on benches. They put cups on them.</i> She put the thing she had seen in the drawer, which did not so much close as simply accept it. There was a good deal in there now. She had stopped being afraid of it. It had the feel of a parcel that you do not open until you are told it is yours.

"I'd like to stay a moment," said her father.

"Take as long as you need."

It was Sophie's sentence. She heard it leave her mouth, and thought: <i>I have caught it.</i> She was surprised how easy it had been to pick up.

They sat. The bees went on. After a while a church clock somewhere above them struck the half hour, and she remembered it from the sandbags, and from the sixth stair, and it sounded entirely different from this side of the water.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Going back, she rowed and he sat and neither of them said anything for nearly a third of the river.

Then he said, "Your mother also said one more thing." He was looking at the stern wake, not at her. "She said that when you said yes, I was not to say <i>good.</i>"

Nora laughed. She could not help it. The oars missed the water and the skiff slewed.

"She said it was a word I used to close doors," said her father. "She said, <i>Say what you mean, for once. Use the other word.</i>" He took a breath. "Thank you, Nora. I'm glad it was you."

She felt it go in. It did not go in like the others, like <i>Anytime</i> or <i>thank you for letting her go</i>, which had needed answers or doors. It simply arrived, and sat down, and was there. She had known all her life that her father loved her. It was only that she had never been told, and it turned out that made no difference whatever and all the difference in the world.

<i>The way a field receives rain,</i> she thought.

"I wanted to," she said. It was entirely true, and she did not have to look for it.

He nodded, once. He turned his face to the water. His hand lay on the gunwale in the sun, and after a moment she saw that one finger was tapping, very lightly, on the wood: a slow, rising, falling little figure, over and over. It went with her stroke. She did not know the tune, though it seemed to her that she ought to have. She glanced at his face, to ask, and found it turned from her, with the corner of the mouth held very carefully still, in the way of a man who has been given something and is taking pains not to spill it.

She decided not to ask.

The landing came up slowly. The shed, the mole, the ferry at her mooring, the elder with its last few flowers going brown. On the post at the end of the mole sat Lotte's gray stone, where it had sat for three weeks. Beside it, new, was a second one, a little smaller, which was not hers, and which she had not put there.

Her father saw her see it.

"It seemed lonely," he said.

On the bench at the top of the mole, in a straw hat, with the sun on her hands and a half-eaten peach beside her, sat Sophie. She stood as the boat grounded. She did not come down. Nora, handing her father out, saw him look up at her over the gunwale, and Sophie, at the top of the mole, give him a very small nod, like a wire pulled taut, and look away at once toward the river.

He walked up the stones past her without a word. At the bench he stopped, and put a hand on the back of it, not on her, and then went on up the path with his hat in his hand.

Nora stood in the shallows with the skiff's line in her fist.

She thought, quite calmly: <i>That's the second time.</i>

She went up the mole. Sophie met her halfway, with her hands open, not reaching.

"How was it?"

"He thanked me," said Nora. "He said it first, in case he didn't manage later."

Sophie put a hand over her mouth, quite suddenly, and turned toward the river, and stood like that for some time with her shoulders square. When she turned back her eyes were bright and her face was steady, and she said, "What did you say?"

"That I wanted to."

"Did you?"

"Yes." Nora looked up at the shed, and at the long low beam over the door, and at the cleft in it where an old card had lain for a year and a half, the truest thing she knew about the whole business. She could see the corner of it from here. A bird. A round hand. <i>You taught me what I wanted.</i>

<i>Forty yards,</i> she thought. He had been afraid of forty yards for thirty-odd years, and then he had put on his Sunday shirt and walked down to the shed and asked. If a man could ask for forty yards, thought Nora, she could ask for a winter.

"Sophie," she said. "There's something I haven't told you. It isn't a small thing. And I think," she said, and it was the hardest sentence she had said yet, harder than <i>I want to,</i> harder than the window, "I think I'm going to have to ask you to hold it with me."

Sophie did not say <i>of course.</i> She did not say <i>anything.</i> She looked at Nora for a long moment, and then, very gently, took the line out of her fist and made it fast to the post, beside two small stones.

"Sunday," said Nora.

"Sunday," said Sophie. "I'll bring the good loaf."

"The one that could hold a door open?"

"That's the one," said Sophie, and put her arm through hers, and they went up the mole together in the long gold light, counting, one half-step apart.

###CHAPTER 12: Sunday

The telephone rang in her apron at nine, which was not an hour her mother used.

"Don't panic," said her mother. She had the voice that laughed at funerals, large and warm and carrying, and Nora, from long habit, held the receiver an inch from her ear. "Nobody's dead. Your father is sitting in my kitchen eating my toast."

Nora sat down on an upturned crate.

"He came in on the seven o'clock bus in his good shirt and rang the bell like a man selling insurance. He's asked me to tell you three things. He's standing here with his hat on his knee going <i>one, two</i> at me like a man in a dentist's chair, so I shall do them in order." A pause, a muffled something, a short laugh. "One: he is staying for supper. Two: he wishes you to know that this is <i>for him.</i> Three, and he says this is the important one: the soup is in the pot."

Nora looked across the shed to the stove. There was a pot on it. She had not noticed it. It was lentil, which was the only thing he made, and the only thing he had never once discussed.

"Why isn't he telling me himself?"

"Because he says if he rings you himself he'll say <i>good</i> and put the phone down." Her mother's voice dropped, though not by much. "I'm told it's a very hard habit to break. Forty years, Nora. I did mention it."

"Mum—"

"I'm not going to say a word about Thursday. I'm going to say <i>thank you for rowing him</i>, and then I'm going to say I'm not asking what the soup is for, because I'm very proud of not asking."

"Nobody asked you to."

"That," said her mother, "is what makes it admirable." And she laughed, so loudly that the telephone buzzed in Nora's hand, and rang off, and Nora sat on the crate for some time with the sun coming through the nine-foot window and the whole shed smelling faintly of lentils, and found that she was smiling like an idiot at a pot.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Sophie came at six, as the church clock was striking. Nora heard it from the mole, where she had been sitting for half an hour not counting anything, and by the fourth stroke she heard the car, and by the sixth she was standing at the head of the lane with her hands in her pockets, as if she were meeting a boat.

Sophie got out with something wrapped in a tea towel. She held it out in both hands, with great ceremony.

"The good loaf."

Nora took it. It was heavy as a paving stone. She tapped it, and it knocked, hollow and flat and very final, like a visitor at a door.

"I made it," said Sophie. "It's the second try. The first went to the ducks, and the ducks declined." Her mouth was steady, but her eyes were not. "I thought it was the right bread for the occasion. Your mother said it was a doorstop."

"It's a doorstop."

"It's an excellent doorstop," said Sophie.

They ate the soup on the threshold of the shed, out of two mismatched bowls, with the big door pushed back and the loaf set against it on the stones, so that it stayed open to the evening. The river was low and gold. The ferry rocked once at her mooring and settled. Over their heads, in the cleft of the beam where the old ferrymen had kept their flood tables, a corner of cream-colored card stuck out, with a little wing drawn on it in blue ink.

Nora did not look up at it. She had not looked up at it, directly, in two years. She knew exactly where it was, the way you know where a stair is in the dark.

She put her bowl down.

"I need to ask you for something," she said. "Before I tell you. I think the asking has to come first, or I won't get to the telling."

Sophie set down her spoon.

"I'm going to tell you about the winter. The one I said was a shut door." It came out level, which surprised her. "I've never told it to anyone. Not Ines. I told it once to a stack of plates. And I can't lift it by myself, Sophie. I've tried for two years. I don't want you to fix it, and I don't want you to be kind about it, not yet, because when people are kind I stop." She swallowed. "I want you to hold the other end."

"Which end?" said Sophie.

It was the oldest question in the shed, and it came out of her so quietly that Nora laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle.

"The one I can't lift."

"Then tell me where to stand."

"There. Where you are." Nora looked at her hands. "Just there."

Sophie laid her own hands open on her knees, palms up, the way she had on the sixth stair, and sat very still. "I'll keep them where you can see them," she said.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

She told it badly, and out of order, and for most of an hour.

She did not tell it the way it had run in her head, with the clean cold shape of a case. It came as it came: the bay window, the thirty-one-year-old woman in the doorway with her coat still on, the smell of linseed in October. <i>I don't know what I'm doing.</i> The night in January, and the eggs. She said the things she had never said, even to the plates, and they were not the things she had expected to say.

"I loved her," she said. "I've never said that. Not out loud, not even to myself in the right voice. It sounds so large. I'd have said I was <i>there</i> for her, I'd have said I was <i>helping.</i> But I loved her, I think, from about the second week of November, and I never told her. I thought it was a thing you gave for nothing. I thought if I named it, it would be a weight she had to carry." A breath. "So I gave it for nothing. And that is exactly what she took."

Sophie did not move.

"She thanked me for being patient," said Nora. "She thanked me for the eggs. She thanked me for all the things she could see. It never occurred to me that the rest of it was there for her to see too, because I'd never let her. I'd been so careful not to ask her for anything that I'd made it impossible for her to give me anything back. She could only be grateful. She didn't know what to be grateful <i>for.</i>" Her voice cracked. "And that's why it felt like being paid. It was the right word to her and the wrong word to me. <i>Thank you.</i> As though I'd been a, a job she'd done well."

There was a long quiet. A swallow came and went under the eaves.

"May I say one thing?" said Sophie. "Not a kind one."

"All right."

"She received what you let her see." Sophie's voice was very low. "She can't have known what you were giving, if you never said what it was. I don't think anyone can receive a gift whose giver is hiding behind it." She looked at her open hands. "That's not her fault. It isn't yours either. It's only what happens. But it does mean that nobody ever got to say yes to <i>you.</i> They got to say yes to a window, and a night, and eggs."

Nora was crying. She had not noticed when it began. It was the quiet kind, as on the fifth stair, with nothing to hold on to.

"And I thought I was being generous."

"You were," said Sophie. "You were being so generous that nobody could reach you." She stopped. Something went across her face, and was gone, and she dropped her eyes and said in a different voice, rougher, "Nobody told you what it was. That's the part I can't—"

She did not finish. Nora, who read hands first, saw that the two open hands on Sophie's knees had closed, once, and opened again, as if the fingers had remembered something.

<i>She's thinking of Lena,</i> thought Nora. <i>Of the bridge. Of all the times she was the one who knew the shape of the thing and the other person didn't.</i> It was an uncommonly tender thing to be sorry about, and Nora filed it, with a warmth that astonished her, under things she would like to put right for her one day.

She did not know yet that it had another label on it.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"That's the winter," she said, at the end. "That's all of it. I've been trying not to be the second winter ever since, and I don't know how, Sophie. That's what I'm really asking. I don't know how to tell, with you, which one this is."

It was the true ask. She heard it come out, small and bare as a stripped frame, with no polish on it, nothing to hold on to. She sat and waited. She was good at waiting. She had been told so.

"I can't tell you the season," said Sophie.

Nora nodded. She had known that. She had braced for it, and it hurt anyway, a small flat ache like a bruise being pressed.

"Nobody honest could. I'd be lying." Sophie's voice shook, and she let it. "But here's what I can do. I will never make you a season without telling you. If I ever know it's a winter, if I ever see the edge of it coming, you will hear it in the <i>first</i> sentence. Not the last. You will never be the last to know what you are to me." She breathed. "That's the only promise I'm sure I can keep. I'd like to make it anyway. Is it enough?"

<i>The first sentence.</i> Nora thought of a bird on a card, and of a flask of coffee, and of the long sun on the mole.

"It's more than anyone's ever offered."

"Can I ask for something now?" said Sophie.

"Yes."

"Will you let me say her name?"

Nora felt her whole body go still. It had been so long that the name was like a coin dropped in a well: something you did not expect to hear land.

"Yes."

"Tessa," said Sophie, very gently.

It was a small round word, with a hiss at the front and a vowel at the back like a held door. Nora braced for it to hurt, and it did, and then it stopped. It was only a name. It belonged to a frightened, brave person who had walked into a shed one October with a window that wouldn't shut. She was somewhere now, with a woman from a gallery, in a house with all its drafts kept out. Nora found, to her great astonishment, that she hoped it was a good house.

"Again," she said.

"Tessa."

"Thank you," said Nora, and heard it. She had not meant to. It had come out of her whole, a word she had not said without a shiver in two years, and she sat very still, and it went by like a bird past a window, and nothing behind it.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"There's a card," said Nora.

"I know. I've been looking at it since half past six." Sophie's mouth quirked. "I didn't want to be the one to say."

"Would you hold the bench?"

It was only a bench, a plank on two blocks, but she stood on it, and it wobbled, and Sophie put both her hands on the end and did not say <i>be careful.</i> Nora reached up into the cleft of the beam and drew down the card. It was warm from the sun. It had gone brown at the edge, and softer than she had expected, like cloth.

She sat down with it on her knee. She had not read it since the day she put it there. She read it aloud, now, for the first time, flatly, in the voice for numbers.

<i>"Thank you for being so patient with me. You taught me what I wanted. I'll never forget this winter."</i>

The river went on. A long way off, a moorhen made its small percussive complaint.

"Do you want to know what I hear?" said Sophie.

"Yes."

"I hear someone who meant every word." She was looking at the card, not at Nora. "That's what makes it hard. If she'd been unkind you could put it down. I think she was grateful, and frightened, and <i>young,</i> in the way that has nothing to do with years. I think she looked at the person who'd made it safe to want, and never once asked whether the person wanted something too." A pause. "You're allowed to keep every word of that and still say it wasn't enough."

Nora held the card.

"I'd have liked," she said slowly, and it was like lifting the last board of a floor, "to have been asked to stay."

It was out. She had never known it was there. It lay in the air between them, small and plain, and there was no door to put it behind.

"Yes," said Sophie, very softly. She did not touch her. She did not say <i>I will.</i> She said only, "Yes. I know."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They sat on while the light went from gold to rose. The soup went cold. In the long grass by the mole the first moths came out, pale as paper.

"I have a door of my own," said Sophie, at last.

Nora turned her head.

"Not a winter. I want to say that first, so you don't think—" She stopped, and began again. "There's a thing I haven't told you. It isn't about anyone I've loved, and it isn't about leaving. I'd like to tell it when you've had a night to sleep on yours. Tonight is <i>yours.</i> I won't spend it." She looked at her hands. "Is that all right? Is it a shut door, or a painted-over one?"

It was Sophie's own phrase, from the dinner in November, handed back across the dusk with a little shaking smile. <i>I'd rather have a shut door than a painted-over one.</i>

<i>Shut,</i> thought Nora. <i>She's told me it's shut.</i> That was the whole of it. It was the very thing she had offered Sophie herself, in November, without knowing what it was worth until it came back.

"Shut," she said. "I can hear you knocking from here."

"I'll knock," said Sophie. "When you want me to."

"Soon, then," said Nora, "because I find I'm very bad at waiting when something's on the other end." She tried a laugh and it nearly came. "I used to think I was good at it. It turns out I was only good at waiting for nothing."

"We'll learn," said Sophie.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

At the end, because it seemed the right thing to do, she carried the card up to the loft.

She went slowly. Sophie came behind with the bowls, and with the loaf under her arm, which she would not leave. The nine stairs took the dusk and gave back an old hollow knock. Nora counted. At the third she heard another count, a half-step behind, a little quieter, taking its time. At the fifth she stopped, and stood on it, and nothing in her gave way. She went on.

The blue drawer stood at the end of the shelf, next to a shoebox of Christmas cards, square and level, where a woman had set it down in the dusk. She slid it open. The glazing points rattled, and the tacks, and the chipped enamel button rolled from one end to the other with its small clear sound. Maren's drawer. A kitchen's drawer. A place for something to go.

She laid the card in, face up, on the tacks, beside the button. She had put it above the door to be rid of it. She laid it here to <i>keep</i> it, on purpose, in a place that wasn't a wound.

<i>It isn't a debt,</i> she thought. <i>It's a place for something to go.</i>

She slid the drawer shut. It closed with a soft, plain sound, like a person agreeing.

Sophie said nothing. She stood on the top stair with a tea towel over her arm and a bread that could hold a door, and looked at Nora with her whole face open, as someone looks at a window at the exact moment the light comes through.

"What?" said Nora.

"Nothing." Sophie's eyes were wet. "You were humming."

"I wasn't."

"All right."

"What was it?"

Sophie shook her head. "I've never been able to say," she said, and the corner of her mouth went up and held, very carefully, like a person who has been given something and is taking pains not to spill it. "I think it's yours."

###CHAPTER 13: Passenger

Sophie's message came at seven on Monday, while Nora was standing at the apartment window, counting nothing.

<i>Knocking. The landing, eleven o'clock. Wear shoes you can sit in a boat in. Your father will row. I'll be on the other side. S.</i>

Nora read it twice. She had slept, alone, in the one good bed over the hardware store, for the first time in a month, and been surprised how well. Sophie had gone home at ten with her tea towel, as agreed, so that she might have a night to sleep on hers.

<i>Your father will row.</i> Her father was due back from town on the ten-forty bus. Nobody had told Sophie that. Nora had not known it herself until her mother had said it on the telephone, twenty minutes ago, in passing, between a remark about the weather and a remark about the soup.

She stood with the telephone in her hand and the street going gold below her. She put the thing where she put such things. She found there was no drawer left to put it in. It lay on top of the others, in plain sight, like a coat on a chair.

<i>All right,</i> she thought. <i>All right. Let's see.</i>

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

He was already in the boat when she came down the lane.

He was sitting in the skiff at the foot of the landing, with the oars shipped and his Sunday hat on, as a man sits in a waiting room who has been waiting a good while and would rather die than be seen to. He had come off the ten-forty bus and gone straight to the boat, without, she suspected, so much as going indoors.

"How was Mum?"

"Loud." He looked at the river. "She sends the soup pot back."

"She hasn't got the soup pot."

"I took it," said her father, "so that I could send it back."

Nora stood in the shallows and looked at him. It was a perfectly sensible sentence and it had no business being funny. She laughed, shortly, and he nodded, once, in the way of a man who has been waiting for exactly that.

"Which end?" she said.

"The stern. You're the cargo."

It was a thing her whole life she had never been. She stepped in, and he held the gunwale out toward her, and she took his hand without waiting for it to be offered. She sat. The boat swayed and steadied. He took up the oars, and pushed off.

Out in the river, she saw what the passenger sees: that you do not face where you are going. She sat with her back to the far bank, looking over her father's shoulder at the landing as it fell away. The shed, the mole, the elder, the ferry riding at her mooring. It was small and neat and entirely itself, like a thing seen in the wrong end of a telescope. She had never seen it from the outside. She thought, <i>That's mine,</i> and then, with a small odd shock, <i>And it's all right.</i>

Her father rowed. He did not talk. Three times he glanced over his shoulder, to take a bearing on the far bank, and each time he did it without searching, as you find a door in your own house in the dark. The third time, she saw his mouth move, once, silently, as if he were counting steps.

<i>He knows exactly where he's going,</i> she thought. <i>He doesn't need to look.</i>

The steps came up under the alder. Six of them, and she saw as they came that they had been scrubbed. Not rinsed. Scrubbed, with a brush, green to gray, so that nothing slimy waited for a foot. The low branch that had hung across the top step last week was gone, cut clean with a saw, the white wound still pale. A new iron ring had been let into the stone, a hand's breadth above the water. A boat hook hung in the fork of the tree, ready to hand.

Her father made fast. He stood, and took her wrist, and she let him.

She said nothing. She did not trust it yet.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

The path went up through the long grass and the dog roses, and she walked it behind him, and she saw things.

She saw that the grass was not long. It was long on either side. Down the middle, from the steps to the gate, ran a line of flattened green, a foot wide, firm, where feet had come and gone. It was not the track of a man who had come once in thirty-odd years. It was a path, with a past.

She saw that one rose had been cut back at the shoulder, so that nothing caught a sleeve.

At the lych-gate she put out her hand and touched the hinge. She did not mean to. It was the oldest reflex she had. Under the moss, along the pin, her thumb came away bright and black and slick with oil. Fresh. A week old, perhaps less. The gate swung without a sound.

<i>She's very good with hinges,</i> Maren had said.

<i>I know,</i> Sophie had said. <i>I've seen them.</i>

"Someone oils this gate," said Nora.

"The vicar," said her father, to the gatepost.

There had not been a vicar at that church in nineteen years. Everyone knew it. Nora looked at the back of his neck, which had gone slowly red.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Sophie was sitting on the bench under the yew, in the straw hat, with her hands folded in her lap, very straight, like a girl at a recital. Beside her on the plank stood a basket. Three tin cups sat on the weathered wood in a row, and a thermos, and a half loaf in a cloth. The cups did not stand at random. Two of them stood on two small dark rings, and the third had been set beside them, at the clean end, with a space left for it, as for a guest at a table.

Her father sat down at the left end of the bench, close to the arm, without being told. Sophie, without looking, poured into the cup in front of him. She turned it, with a small neat half-turn, so that the handle came to his hand. He did not say thank you. He did not need to. It was the way you pass something you have passed a hundred times.

It was the sort of thing Nora saw at once. She read hands first.

"Nora," said Sophie. She had gone white to the lips. "Thank you for coming. I said I'd knock, and I'm knocking, and I want to tell you what's behind the door. I thought it would be better if your father—"

"Don't," said Nora.

It came out quite quietly. It was not a loud word. Sophie stopped as if she had been touched.

"I'll say it," said Nora. "Let me. I'm good at reading things. I'd like to read this one."

She stood in the long grass at the edge of the yew's shade, with her hands at her sides and nothing in them.

"The church road," she said. "In the winter, when I was putting in the window sash. <i>I'll go round by the church road,</i> you said, as if you'd driven it a hundred times, and you had never been on this side of the river in your life. And there was a look. Between you and my father, over my head, like a wire pulled taut."

Neither of them moved.

"In the spring, driving home, we passed the turn for the church road, and nobody said a word. Three forks in a paper sack, on the day of the flood, and a bed made up in the back room that nobody had slept in since my mother, and a thermos that went to you, not to me." Her voice was very level. "A supper at six, offered at noon, when I'd said nothing. <i>Say stop and I'll stay home.</i> And you came at six. At six exactly. At the exact hour that I—" She stopped. "You knew. On the Wednesday, at noon, you knew I'd say <i>fine, busy.</i> You knew I'd sit with the telephone and delete the word. You came at six because someone had told you it would be six."

Sophie's mouth opened.

"And the hinge. And the steps. And that." She nodded at the plank, at the rings, at the three cups. "That's not a bench people sit on. That's a table. You've sat here. Both of you. A great many times."

Silence. A bee moved over the clover. Down at the water's edge, a coot made its small clicking complaint.

"How long?" said Nora.

Her father put down his cup.

"The eleventh of November," he said. "A Tuesday. It was raining."

He looked at his hands, which lay open on his knees.

"I wrote your marks in the ledger that day. The number was in the back, in pencil. I wasn't looking. I was looking for a pencil." His voice was the flat one, the voice for figures, and under it something was working very hard. "I stood in the kitchen. I'd had the telephone for forty years and not once rung a person I didn't know. I rang."

"What did you say?"

"I said, <i>I am the ferryman. I am Nora's father. I don't know what I'm telephoning to ask.</i>"

"And what did she say?"

"She said, <i>Is she all right?</i>" He swallowed. "And I said, <i>She's humming.</i>"

Nora felt the ground go out a little, very gently, under the soles of her feet.

"You hum," said her father. "When the tea's about right. You've done it since you were five, when you thought no one could hear. You stopped for two winters. You'd started again that week." He cleared his throat. "I had nobody to tell. Your mother knew, but she'd have wept, and I couldn't have borne it. I thought: there is a person who has made my daughter hum. I thought, I would like her to know that someone had noticed."

It was so small and so large that she could not look at it.

"I don't hum," she said, stupidly.

"You do," said Sophie and her father together.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"Nobody told me," said Nora.

It came out of her low and strange. She had not known that it was coming. It was the sentence at the very bottom of the drawer, the one she had been sitting on for two years.

"Nobody told me what it was. Eight months. Twenty-seven—how many? Twenty-seven Tuesdays, you've been sitting on this bench drinking tea about me, the two of you, and I've been, I've been <i>on the other bank,</i> and everyone knew what it was except me." She was shaking. "That's what it was. That's what Tessa was. Everyone knew it was a winter except the one person who was in it."

"Yes," said Sophie.

She had not got up. She had not said <i>it isn't the same.</i> She sat on the bench, with her hat in her lap and the tears standing in her eyes, and took it as a person takes a wave.

"You're right," she said. "It's the same wound. I'd have given a great deal for it not to be. I flinched last night when you said it, because I knew it was coming, and I knew I could say nothing, and I'd rather have cut my hand."

"You <i>knew,</i>" said Nora. "Last night. You sat on my threshold and ate my father's soup and let me tell you about Tessa, and you <i>knew.</i>" Her voice cracked. "You said, <i>You will never be the last to know what you are to me.</i>"

"I did."

"And I was the last to know."

"For eight months," said Sophie, "you were." And she did not look away.

It was so exactly the right thing to say that Nora hated her for it. She turned from the bench. She walked, quickly, with her hands at her sides, down the flattened path through the roses to the top of the stone steps, and stood on the first one with the river in front of her, brown-green and slow, and shook.

She did not hear them follow. She did not hear anything for a long time but the river.

And then, because she was a person who read hands, and had no hands to read, she did what she had never done in her life. She turned and looked back.

They had not moved. They sat at either end of the bench, her father at the left, Sophie at the right, with the empty place between them and the third cup shining in it. And they had both, slowly, turned their backs.

Not to her. To the churchyard. Two backs, square, like bookends. A man in a Sunday hat with his shoulders set like a man at a graveside, and a woman in a straw hat with her head a little bowed, as someone sits who has decided to wait for a train.

<i>I'll wait,</i> she thought, and heard it, in another voice, from the bottom step of a loft.

It was a door. It was the kind she had held for others, standing at the end of a corridor looking at the wall. Two people who loved her were holding it for her now, with their backs turned, so that she might be unobserved.

She found that she was crying again. It did not feel like a defeat.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

She stood a long time on the steps.

She looked into the drawer. It stood open, as it had for months, and she looked, for once, at what was in it.

A supper at six, offered at noon, to a woman who had deleted <i>yes please</i> with her own thumb. A bed made up in a back room, on a Monday, by a man who had not made a bed in nine years, for a night that might never come. A thermos handed to the wrong person, so that the right one would know she was not alone. Three forks. A name written in a ledger in pencil. A father, standing in a kitchen with a telephone, saying <i>She's humming</i> to a stranger, because he had nobody else.

<i>Not for return,</i> she thought. <i>To delight.</i>

Every single thing in the drawer was a hand held out. And none of them had been held out in order to be taken. They had been held out <i>so that she might take them, or not.</i>

And yet. She had not been asked. That was not nothing. It stood in her chest like a stone in a stream: round, hard, hers.

She was allowed to have both.

<i>Something can be true, and it can be a gift, and it can still have been done to you.</i> Nora took a long breath of river air. It tasted of silt and green.

She went back up the path.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

"I'm still angry," she said.

Sophie turned, at once, with her whole face. Her father turned a moment after, slowly, like a man waking.

"I want to say it out loud and have you hear it. Not fix it. Not make it kinder than it is." She stood at the edge of the shade, with her hands loose. "I'm angry that you did it behind my back. I'm angry that I was the last to know. I'm angry that I've spent eight months as a, a window you were looking through, and nobody—" She stopped. "You could have asked me."

There was a short, ringing silence.

"You'd have said you could manage," said Sophie and her father, in the same breath.

They stopped, and looked at each other, and Sophie put her hand over her mouth.

Nora stood quite still. She heard it again in the air, like a thing struck on a bell: <i>You'd have said you could manage.</i> She thought of a doorway, and a sandbag, and a smooth stone of a sentence on her tongue.

<i>I've got it.</i>

She laughed. It broke from her all at once, hiccuping, appalled, a dreadful wet helpless laugh that bent her in the middle. "I would have," she said. "God help me. I'd have said, <i>I've got it, thank you, I'm fine.</i> I'd have said it to both of you. In the same voice."

"The very same," said her father, and his face did the thing.

"Don't," said Nora, laughing and crying. "Don't you dare. I'm not finished being angry."

"No," said Sophie, shakily. "Of course not."

"Say the rest."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Sophie did not stand. She sat with her hat in her lap and her hands around it, and she said it as one says a thing one has practiced in the dark.

"I couldn't get through your door," she said. "That's the whole of it. I tried the front way, every day, with everything I had. I said, <i>Do you want me near or far?</i> And you said <i>near.</i> That was mine, Nora, he never told me to ask that, I want you to know. But I couldn't ask <i>everything</i> the front way, because you couldn't answer it. You'd have said <i>fine.</i> So I went round to the side, and found he was already standing at the side, with a bucket and a ladder and no way to get in either. And we looked at one another." She gave a small, shaken laugh. "Two people leaning on a door that opens inwards, each so afraid of pushing that we leaned instead. And he'd say, <i>She likes the last of the bread.</i> And I'd say, <i>She counts when she's frightened.</i> And we'd write it down, in our heads, and then go away and put it where you'd find it. A supper at six. A bed. A back turned on the stairs." She looked up. "Everything I did that you didn't know about was one of two things: something you'd have said yes to if you'd known it was allowed to want, or nothing at all. I never once made you do a thing. I made <i>room.</i> And every time I made it, you stepped in. Every time. Your hands. Your choice."

"You did it without asking."

"Yes."

"You hid it."

"I did." Her eyes did not leave Nora's. "I hid it because I was afraid. Not of you leaving. Of you being kind about it. That you'd say <i>of course, if it helps you,</i> and take it from me like a present and never once want it. I wanted you to <i>find</i> it. I know what that makes me. I know it's the same shape as a secret. I told myself I'd tell you when you could bear it, and that is the very sentence that grows a wall." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "It was a shut door, and I told you so. But I'd stopped knocking and called it patience, and I'm sorry, Nora. I'm sorrier than I can say. I'd change the hiding. I wouldn't change the loving. I can't make those two come apart, and I'm sorry for that too."

"I'm not sorry I told her things," said her father.

He had not turned his face. He spoke to the yew, in the voice for numbers.

"I'm sorry I did it behind your back. I'd be a liar if I said the other. I had a daughter I could not ask. I'd tried for thirty years. I could stand in a doorway and say <i>Do you want—</i>, and you'd say <i>I've got it,</i> and I'd go away." He drew breath. "And I thought: there is somebody here who can say it to her. Who can say all of it. The things I had no words for. I haven't got the words, Nora. I've got the numbers. I've got <i>four inches since Friday.</i> It was the only way I knew to say the thing."

"Which thing?"

He turned then. His eyes were red, and his hat was in his hands, and his mouth was working.

"That I love you," said her father, quite quietly. "That I'd walk across the whole river if I could. That all my life I've been afraid to put one foot on the water and ask you for anything, in case you gave it."

The coot clicked. Somewhere over the church, a bell, one note. The clock. Eleven.

Nora crossed the grass.

She did not remember doing it. She was just there, in front of the bench, and then she was sitting on it, in the empty place between them, in the exact middle, with the third tin cup cold at her elbow and her father's sleeve against her left side and Sophie's against her right. She did not touch either of them. She sat, and breathed, and was held on both sides by two people who had made a hole in a wall and waited for her to climb through.

"I'm still angry," she said.

"Yes," said Sophie.

"I might be for some days."

"I'll bring the loaf."

"It's a doorstop."

"I'm told it's very good for a door."

Nora laughed. She lifted the third cup and drank from it. It was lukewarm, and strong, and it tasted exactly like the cup of tea you are handed by someone who knows you.

"New rule," she said, into the cup.

They waited.

"Whatever you do. Whatever the two of you do about me. I want to be told. Not asked. <i>Told.</i> After is acceptable. Before is better." She breathed. "And the next time, I'd like to be invited. I'd like a cup. I'd like to be on the bench."

"A cup," said her father. His voice had gone odd.

"Tuesdays?" said Sophie, very carefully, to the air.

"Tuesdays," said Nora. "And Mum's in on it."

Her father said nothing. He looked steadily at the churchyard wall.

"Dad."

"Since the first week," said her father. "She's been unbearable."

"She said she was proud of not asking."

"She was asked," said her father, with immense dignity, "a great deal. By me. Every Wednesday. For eleven weeks."

And that was what did it. Nora put the cup down on the plank in its ring, and put her face in her hands, and laughed until she could not breathe, and felt the bench shake on either side of her, because she was not the only one.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

When the laughing had gone, and the tea was cold, she said, "There's something else."

They looked at her.

"It's not for today. Today I'm angry." She turned the cup in its ring, a quarter-turn, and a quarter again. "I told you I'd like to find out. I would. I'm going to ask you properly. It has a boat in it, and a bank, and a long time. It's going to take more than a bench."

Sophie did not breathe.

"I'll be here," she said, "when you're not angry."

"I know," said Nora.

"I'm not in any hurry."

"I know." She almost smiled. "That's the maddening thing."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Sophie went back by the church road. She had the car on the far side, in the lane behind the lych-gate, and she kissed Nora once, quite lightly, on the corner of the mouth, and did not say <i>are we all right.</i> She said, "Tomorrow, then, at the bench?" and Nora said, "Tomorrow, at the shed," and Sophie nodded and went up the path with her straw hat in her hand.

Her father rowed her home.

She sat in the stern, with her face to the landing she was leaving, her back to the water she was going toward, and the sun full on the river between. She was a passenger. She had never been one. It had seemed to her once like a diminishing, like being carried, like being a thing in somebody's arms. It was not that at all. It was only that somebody else had the oars, and she was allowed to watch the light.

At the foot of the steps, small as a figure in a stained-glass window, Sophie had come back down. She stood on the bottom stone with her hand over her eyes. She had not gone. She was waiting to see the boat land, as one waits at a door for the sound of the key.

"She's still there," said Nora.

"I know." Her father did not turn his head. "I can feel it from here."

<i>All my life,</i> thought Nora, <i>I was the one on the bank.</i> She had stood at the end of the mole at dawn and dusk for thirty years with a lantern, so that someone, anyone, might know where the crossing was. <i>Someone has to.</i> She had thought that was the whole of what a person could be. She had not known that it was only half. The other half was this: to be on the water, with your back to where you are going and your face to the one who waits, and to feel, for the first time, the whole width of the river between you shine like a thing that was meant.

She lifted her hand.

On the far bank, a small figure in a straw hat lifted hers.

Between them, silver and wide and in no particular hurry, the river went on carrying them both.

###CHAPTER Epilogue I: The Ferry Crosses Itself

Nora hummed.

She did it at first light on the last Saturday in October, on the mole, with a mug of tea going cold between her palms and the river smoking in the cold, and she did not know she was doing it. It was only when she stopped, to blow on the tea, that she heard it: not the tune but the shape the tune had left in the air. A small rising, falling figure, like a thing set down. She heard it the way you hear a clock only when it stops.

She sat very still.

<i>I don't hum,</i> she had said, in a churchyard in July, with her father and the woman she loved telling her otherwise. She had gone on not humming for three months out of sheer stubbornness, and apparently humming all the while.

She tried to find it again. It would not come. When she went looking for it, it went, like a bird off a fence. She laughed, quietly, to the river, and the heron on the far bank lifted one foot and put it down.

There was no bell at the landing. There never had been. The old ferrymen had hung a lantern on the post at dusk, and kept their ears open, and whoever wanted the crossing came down to the bank and was fetched. Nobody had ever been hurried. Michaelmas daisies stood in the mole-grass, pale violet, late, and there were rosehips in the hedge. The elder had given up its leaves. The bench at the head of the mole, a long plank on two stumps, shone in the first sun like the inside of a shell, worn so smooth by years of waiting that it was warm to the hand.

Sophie came down the lane at seven with a loaf.

It was a proper one now. She had stopped making doorstops in August, with a small ceremony. She held it out in its tea towel and said, "I'm early. You said eight. I couldn't sleep, I've been awake since four, and I thought if I waited in the car any longer I'd start the engine and go to Norway."

"I said eight because I have to tell you something first."

Sophie stopped.

"Not a bad thing. It's a <i>told, not asked.</i>" Nora put the mug down. She was aware of her pulse in her wrists. "I've been conspiring."

Sophie looked at her for a moment. Then, slowly, her whole face lit.

"Who with?"

"My father. My mother. Ines. Mara and Lena on the telephone, nine times, Lena's been insufferable. Maren. The ladies from under the oak. Your mother and her wife." She took a breath. "It's this afternoon. There's a table in the glebe meadow behind the church, across the river. Everyone's coming. I'm telling you before, because that's the rule, and because I'd like you to be able to say no to the whole thing, up to the moment you'd have to walk into it."

"What is it?"

"That's the second thing. I'm not going to hold a party for a question I haven't asked yet." Nora stood up. "Will you come across with me? On the ferry. It's the only way I can ask it."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

She had worked that ferry for fourteen years and never once ridden her as a passenger.

She was a flat old craft, black-seamed and broad, with a rail around her and a bench down either side, and she crossed the way ferries of her kind have always crossed: slantwise to the current, on a long wire strung from a post upstream. There was no motor. There was no oar. You cast off, and set the rudder to the angle, and stepped back; and then the river itself took her by the shoulder and carried her over, in a long slow diagonal, with the wire singing very faintly overhead like a held note.

All you did was set the angle, and then there was nothing to do.

Nora cast off. She set the rudder. She sat down on the bench beside Sophie, with her hands open on her knees, and the ferry swung out from the mole and gave herself to the river.

"There's nothing to do," said Sophie, surprised.

"No. I used to think this was the part where you worked." Nora watched the mooring slide away. "It isn't. It's the part where you're carried."

The light came up the water. The wire sang. Mid-river, where the current ran deepest and the shed behind them was the size of a matchbox, Nora took a breath.

"I'm going to ask in three parts," she said. "You can answer any of them, or none. The last two are only a way of saying the first, and I couldn't think how to say it all at once."

Sophie did not breathe.

"One." Her voice shook. She let it. "Will you marry me?"

Sophie closed her eyes.

"Two. I told you I'd like to find out. About a child. I don't know that I want one. I might. I want it not to be a decision, Sophie, I want it to be a question we keep. Out loud. For years, if we need to. Until we both know." She swallowed. "I never knew it was allowed to be a question. I'd like to find out with you. Not instead of being married. After. Beside."

A coot made its small noise, far off. The ferry swung.

"Three. The far bank." Nora gestured at the shore coming up, gold and brown, the churchyard wall, the yew, the roof of the vicarage over the trees. "That house has stood empty for nineteen years. It has two windows that face the river, and two that face the church, and every one of them needs mending. I'd like to mend them. I'd like to live in it, on that side, with you. And keep the shed, on this. I'd like to cross every morning, and cross again every night, and be a person who goes both ways." Her voice dropped. "You said once you'd been all crossing. I said I'd always been the one who stays where the crossing is. I think we've both been half of it. I'd like us to be the whole river."

She stopped. It was out. It lay in the air between them, small and plain and unguarded as a stripped frame, with nothing to hold on to.

Sophie opened her eyes.

They were full. She did not wipe them. She sat on the old bench with the river going by on either side, and said, in a voice with the last of her laughter in it:

"Which end?"

And Nora, who had meant to be solemn, put her hands over her face and laughed until the ferry rocked.

"Yes," said Sophie. "Yes. Yes, in the first sentence. Yes to the one, and yes to the one that's a question, and yes to the house with the awful windows." She took Nora's wrists, gently, and drew her hands away from her face. "I said I'd never stay on either side long enough to find out what it was like. I'd like to stay on both. I've wanted to for so long that I stopped being able to say it. Thank you for asking me."

"Thank you for—" Nora began, and stopped, and heard it. "I don't have to finish that."

"No."

"I'll say it in the sixth sentence, then."

"Do," said Sophie. "I'd like to be told."

They sat in the middle of the river, while the wire sang, and the far bank came up slowly to meet them, unhurried, in a fall of yellow leaves.

"May I ask for something?" said Sophie, after a time.

"Yes."

"Teach me to row. Properly." She smiled, wet-eyed. "I want to be able to bring you home."

###CHAPTER Epilogue II: The Table in the Meadow

The glebe meadow lay behind the churchyard wall, on the slope toward the water, and in October it was all the colors of a bonfire gone gentle: dry grass the color of bread, knapweed gone to seed, the last scabious holding up its pale blue pincushions to the sun. Somebody had mown a long straight path through it. At the end of the path stood a table.

It was made of doors. Nora had taken ten from the keeping wall's old reserve, paneled doors, plank doors, a pantry door with its brass finger-plate still on, and laid them flat on trestles end to end, so that the whole length of the table was a kind of long argument against waste. Down the middle ran jam jars of Michaelmas daisies and rosehips, candles in them waiting for dusk. Chairs had come from every kitchen in three counties. None matched. There were forty-one.

The ferry had been going to and fro since noon.

She came across on the current, empty, and came back loaded, and went again. Between crossings, her father, in his Sunday shirt, rowed the skiff. He rowed anyone who preferred it, one at a time, and he had asked that it be so. He had said it at supper on Tuesday, in the voice for numbers: "I'd like to row them. It's for me." Nobody had argued.

Mara and Lena came first.

They stepped off the ferry at one o'clock, Mara quiet, with a tray in her arms, and Lena already talking. Nora was standing at the top of the steps. She had not planned what she would do. She held out her hands, open, and Mara put the tray into them.

It was a cold frame. A small one, a few inches deep, made from old window sash with a hinged glass lid, and under the glass in a bed of dark soil there were seedlings, green as the first day of spring. They were chard, and lettuce, and something with feathery leaves.

"It's from the oak," said Mara. "The same wood. The same glass. We thought the garden would want one."

Nora looked at it. She thought of a field and of rain. She had spent most of her life saying <i>you shouldn't have</i> to things like this, in a voice like a door.

"Thank you," she said. "I want it."

Mara's face did what Maren's had done in June: opened, all the way down, like a window thrown wide.

"There it is," said Lena, behind her, thickly. She had her fist against her mouth.

"Thank you for letting her come," Nora said to her, then, and it was the thing she had said in the spring in a different order. "And thank you for letting her stay."

Lena took her by both shoulders and shook her once, hard, and did not say a word, which for Lena was a speech.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

The rest came all afternoon, like weather.

Her mother stepped off the third load, in a green coat, round and loud and laughing at something nobody had said. She took one look at Sophie and stopped on the steps.

"So you're the one," said her mother, in the voice that carried to the church. "My husband has been telephoning me about you for eleven months. Every Wednesday. I've had to <i>listen.</i>"

"I'm told you were unbearable," said Sophie.

Her mother roared. It went off across the water in a flock of startled ducks. She caught Sophie in a hug that lifted her off the grass, and over Sophie's shoulder met Nora's eye with something wet and fierce and entirely unashamed.

"I'd like to ask for something," her mother said, setting her down. "I've asked for nothing for myself in forty years. I've asked for bread and a doorstop and Sunday. And a man with a hat, which I got." She took breath. "I'd like a cup. On Tuesdays. On that bench by the yew."

Nora's father, standing six feet off with the skiff's painter in his hand, went the color of the setting sun.

"That," said Nora, "is not mine to give. It's his."

"I haven't got four cups," said her father, hoarsely.

"Then you'll have to get a fourth."

"I'll get a fourth," said her father. And, after a moment, to the grass, "Good."

"Dad."

"Thank you," said her father. "I mean thank you."

Maren came with Ruth and Lotte on the fourth load. Lotte was in her yellow rain hat, though it was not raining, and she walked straight off the ferry and onto the meadow and up to Nora and held out a drawing.

It was a boat with a very large sail. Across the bottom, in her mother's hand, was written <i>LOTTE SAYS,</i> and then, in a different hand, a small, careful, wobbling one, a long line of capitals.

<i>NORA.</i>

"I did it," said Lotte, with enormous gravity. "That's you."

"That's me," said Nora. She knelt in the grass, and took the drawing, and could not for a moment say anything else.

Over her head, Maren and Sophie had found each other. Maren touched Sophie's shoulder lightly, with two fingertips, as one touches a forehead. "Hinges," she said.

"I've oiled them all," said Sophie, gravely.

"I know. I saw."

Ruth had put her hands in her back pockets. "Where do I put the sister?" she said, to nobody in particular.

"What sister?" said Ines.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Ines had arrived at nine that morning, with a toolbox and a face like a closed door, and by eleven she had carried more furniture across a meadow than the entire county had in a decade. She wore a good coat and old boots. She had said "Anyone?" four times in Nora's hearing since breakfast, in the voice she used when she meant <i>nobody has asked me anything, and I will not mention it.</i>

Nora had been waiting for the right moment. In the end there was not one; there was only a morning, and a door.

It was the last of the ten, the pantry door, stubborn and heavy and awkward at the corners. They had both got to the end of the path with it, and set it down against the trestles to rest. Ines was breathing hard, with her hands on her knees. Nora looked at her. Fourteen years. A friend of eighteen, stout-hearted, standing at the end of every job with her sleeves rolled up saying <i>Want me to hold the other end?</i> and being told <i>I've got it.</i>

"Ines," she said.

"Mm."

"Would you hold the other end of this?"

Ines did not move.

She looked up very slowly. She looked at Nora's face, and then at the door, and then at her own two hands, which had hung at her sides in a thousand rooms with nothing in them, and Nora saw the whole of it cross her friend's face at once: the lentils, the lifts, the nights, the long patient unrefused offer.

"Fourteen years," said Ines. Her mouth was shaking. "Fourteen <i>years,</i> you stubborn woman."

"I know."

"I was going to be so dignified about this."

"I know."

"Bloody hell." Ines wiped her face with both fists, like a child. Then she squared her shoulders, and stooped, and took the end of the door in both hands, with the care of a woman lifting a baby. "Which way?"

"Toward the table."

"I know which way," said Ines, and wept, and carried it.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

They sat down at four.

The light had gone long and low and honey-colored across the grass, and all forty-one chairs were filled. Nora sat in the middle of one side of the table, with Sophie on her right and her father on her left, and she looked down the length of it, at the faces, and could not at first take it in. Maren and Ruth and Lotte, Lotte on three telephone books. Ines in the place of honor, red-eyed and wholly happy, with Ruth's sister beside her, a calm woman with her hands in her pockets, who had turned out to be extremely good at listening. Mara and Lena. Sophie's mother and her mother's wife, who had come off the ferry holding hands and a trifle. Six women from under the oak, who had arrived on one load not knowing a soul but their hosts and by half past three seemed to know everyone: Rachel and Miriam, Claire and Elena, Clara and Elise, passing plum cake. Her own mother, in the green coat, laughing. And, pinned along the end of the nearest door with brass drawing pins, two postcards.

The first was of a harbor, blue and white. <i>I never said thank you for the window. I waited on the stairs for the wrong sentence. I'm so glad you said the right one. H.</i>

The second was a card with a bird. It was the reply to one Nora had sent in August, a drawing of a window, with six lines under it, written four times and finally not torn up. It said, in a round hand, in two words: <i>It held.</i>

She had put them both there herself, that morning, with the pins. They were not hers to be rid of. They were hers to keep.

Her father stood.

He did it with great ceremony. He took off his hat and set it on the table, and held on to the back of his chair, and there was a hush down the doors.

"I have two things," he said, in the flat voice. "The river's eleven inches under the line. That is a good height. That's the first."

A ripple. He waited.

"The second I've been practicing for thirty-seven days." He drew breath. "Forty years ago I asked my wife whether I might have an hour in the evening to sit on a bench. She said yes. I have not asked her for much since. I am told it is a failing." Down the table, Nora's mother put her hand over her eyes. "I would like to say that on the eleventh of November last, I went into a kitchen to look for a pencil. And I came out with a daughter-in-law." He looked at Sophie. His mouth did the thing. "It is the best piece of stationery I have ever owned."

The meadow went up in a roar. Her mother had to be hit on the back. Sophie had both hands over her face and was shaking, quite helplessly, with a laughter that had a great deal else in it.

"That's all," said her father, and sat down, and then stood again, absurdly, as if remembering. "Thank you," he said. "To all of you. For coming. I wanted to say it myself."

Lena was on her feet before he had sat. She had a glass in her hand and a look about her of a woman who had been told she was only to say three sentences.

"A year ago," said Lena, "I called this one a bridge." She pointed at Sophie. "I was right. She is. She has crossed more rivers than any person I know." She turned the glass. "I said to Nora once that she was a landing. Somebody on the other side who isn't going anywhere. I'd like to retract that."

Nora looked up.

"The thing about a landing is that it waits," said Lena, and her voice shook, and she let it. "It stands on its side and waits for the boat. Nora doesn't wait. She <i>crosses.</i> And the thing about a bridge is, it needs somewhere to end. I never had the right word for what I wanted for this one, and it turns out it was <i>a bank on both sides.</i> Two banks. One river." She lifted the glass. "To the landing, then. Both of them."

"The landing," said forty people.

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

Ines spoke last. She stood without being asked, which she'd never done in her life, and held on to the edge of the pantry door for support.

"I've known her fourteen years," said Ines. "I've had a great deal to say about her in that time, most of it in my head. She's the most infuriating, the most stubborn, the most carefully wonderful person I know. She doesn't ask. Everything I ever did for her, I did against her better judgement. I carried her groceries. I carried her <i>lentils.</i>" The table laughed. "I stood in her shed for a decade with my hand out, going <i>want me to hold the end?</i> And she said <i>I've got it.</i> I want everyone to know I took it personally."

"I know," said Nora, across the table, wetly. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be. She asked me this morning." Ines's face did something complicated. "It took fourteen years. It was a door. It was a very heavy door, and I'd have carried it to Norway." She drew breath. "I'm very good at ends. I'd like it noted. Next time I'd like to be handed a middle."

"I can arrange that," said Ruth's sister, to her plate.

Ines turned the color of the rosehips. Down the table, Ruth put both hands over her face.

"Anyone?" said Ines, faintly. And everybody who had ever heard her say it, which was nearly everybody, answered together, a little drunk, in unison, with a roar that put up every bird in the churchyard:

"<i>Yes!</i>"

###CHAPTER Epilogue III: The Other Sentence

They went home in the dark, in the last load, on a river as flat as a plate.

The lanterns were lit on both banks. Her father rowed the skiff, with her mother in the stern, wrapped in the green coat, singing something very loudly and not well. Maren and Ruth had gone on the earlier ferry with the sleeping Lotte, who had one hand around a smooth gray stone she'd refused to be parted from. The oak women were in a bus. Lena, at the water's edge, had said "Thank you for letting her stay," once more, to Nora, very quietly, and Nora had said, "Thank you for letting her come." It was beginning to be a sort of liturgy. She was prepared to let it.

At the landing the mole stood white under the lantern at the end of the post. Sophie had gone ahead to see to the door. Her father had gone up the path with his wife on his arm. They were talking about the soup.

Nora was alone in the shed.

She did the cups, because it seemed a thing to do. There were thirty or so, tins and china and jam jars, brought across by the dozen and sent back by the dozen, and she stood at the old stone sink under the nine-foot window with her sleeves pushed up and the tap running and washed them one at a time. The windows were black. A moth worked at the lamp. She was so tired that her bones hummed, and so happy that the tiredness felt like a kind of weather, and she did not think of anything at all.

Above her, in the kitchen, her father began to whistle.

It came down through the old boards like water through sand. A low, unhurried little tune, a rising figure and a falling one, over and over, with a place in the middle where it paused as if to let someone else in. He did it badly. He had always whistled badly, thinly, between his teeth, the sound of a man who does not know he is being heard.

She had known it all her life.

She stood quite still with a cup in her hand. Water ran down her wrist. And she heard, to her great astonishment, that she was no longer silent. Somewhere below the whistle, quiet and warm, under her breath, her own voice had come in, in the place in the middle, on exactly the right note.

She stopped. Overhead, so did he.

For a long time neither of them moved. The moth struck the lamp. The tap ran.

She went up the stairs.

He was standing in the kitchen in the lamplight in his shirtsleeves with a dishcloth in his hand, and his face, as she came through the door, was the face of a man who has been caught at something and is deciding how much to be sorry for.

"It's yours," said Nora.

"It's <i>yours,</i>" said her father. "I've been hearing it for twenty-seven years. You started it on the mole. You were five. I thought it was a thing you'd made up. I never asked." He swallowed. "I was afraid that if I asked, you'd stop."

"I got it from you."

He stared at her.

"You whistled it. At dusk. When you came down to take the lantern in. I used to sit on the bench and wait for it." She laughed, helplessly, with her hand on the door frame. "I thought it was the river. I thought it was how the evening <i>sounded.</i> I've been humming it for twenty-seven years and I thought it was mine."

"I thought it was yours," said her father. "I thought you'd been <i>given</i> it."

"By who?"

"The river," said her father, with his face working. "I suppose."

They stood in the kitchen, in the warm light, the ferryman and his daughter, two people with one tune and neither of them able to say who had begun it. It was, she thought, entirely possible that no one had. It had been there before either of them. It had simply passed from throat to throat down the whole long line of people who had stood on that mole at dusk, waiting for a boat to come in or a person to cross. It was the sound of waiting, when the waiting was not hard.

"What's it called?" said Nora.

"I never knew."

"Then it hasn't got a name."

"No."

"Then it's ours."

He did not say <i>good.</i> He put down the dishcloth, and crossed the floor in two steps, and took his daughter in his arms, in a clumsy, rigid, forty-year hug, with his chin on the top of her head and his hat for once nowhere in sight. She stood inside it. She had learned how. <i>Arms are a thing you have to learn to be inside.</i> It took a moment, and then it was the easiest thing in the world.

"Thank you," said Nora, into his shirt.

"For what?"

"For asking."

"Thank <i>you,</i>" said her father, hoarsely, "for being asked."

<span class="scene-break">· · ·</span>

It was nearly midnight when she came down to the mole.

The bench was empty, shining in the lantern light. Beyond it the river lay black and wide and still. Sophie was sitting at the far end of the plank with her shoes off, wearing Nora's old coat, with the flask of tea between her feet. She had been there a while. She had not turned her head at the sound on the planks.

"They've gone to bed," said Nora, sitting down. "Both of them. In separate rooms. They're calling it convenient."

"Of course they are."

"He's put a fourth cup out, on the sill. I saw."

"Good," said Sophie, and laughed, and it was a good laugh, low and unsteady, with no hook in it.

Nora took the tea and drank. It was just right.

They sat and did not say anything for some time. There was a wind in the willows. The lantern swung, a little, on its post. Across the dark water, on the far bank, a single lantern burned on the steps where the ferry had been left, and above it, black against the stars, stood the roof of the vicarage. Nora looked at it, and thought of two windows, and of the house with the bad sashes, and of all the long years of mending that lay ahead like a road in good weather.

"You're humming," said Sophie.

"I know," said Nora.

"You <i>know?</i>"

"I can hear it." She smiled into the dark. "It's my father's. I'm going to teach it to you. I think it needs a third voice."

"I'd like that."

Out on the river, something moved.

It was the ferry. She had been left on the far side, on the wire, with her lantern lit, and somewhere in the last hour the current had taken her by the shoulder and she had come over by herself. Nobody was aboard. Nobody had cast her off. She came across the dark slantwise and slow, in no hurry, with the wire singing very faintly overhead like a held note, and her one small light gold on the water.

Nora watched her come.

There was no bell. There never had been. Whoever wanted the crossing, wanted it. Whoever waited, waited. The bench at the head of the mole had been worn smooth by years of it, by thousands of people sitting just here with their hands on their knees and their eyes on the water, hoping a boat would come. The plank was warm under her palm.

Tonight, for the first time in the hundred and ninety years of the landing, no one was waiting. They were only there, the two of them, side by side, when it came.

The ferry touched the mole with a sound like a held breath let go. The lantern rocked, and was still.

"Shall we?" said Nora.

"Which end?" said Sophie.

And she took her hand, and they stepped aboard together, and the river bore them over.
