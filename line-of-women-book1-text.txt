###CHAPTER 1: Prologue — The Dream in the Mist
Before she ever saw her face, Eleonore had already followed her once, in a country that existed only behind closed eyes.

In the dream, the forest was always the same: birches pale as bone, mist pooling ankle-deep between the roots, the hush of a world holding its breath. Eleonore walked barefoot through it without cold, without fear, drawn forward by a silhouette that moved always a little ahead of her — a woman's shape, dark-haired, unhurried, never quite turning around. She could not have said how she knew it was a woman, or why her chest ached at the sight of that unturning back, only that some part of her recognized the shape the way one recognizes a voice calling one's own name from another room.

She never caught her. She never once saw her face. But each time the dream came, she woke with the same words trembling on her lips, spoken to no one: wait for me.

On the morning this story begins, she woke with those words still warm in her mouth, her heart beating as though she had run some great distance, though she had not moved from her bed. Outside her window, the sky over Castle Albrecht was the pale grey of a held breath, dawn not yet decided whether to break clear or grey.

She rose, lit her candle though the room was already growing light, and opened the small book in which she kept the things she did not say aloud.

I dreamed of her again, she wrote. I do not know her name. I do not know that she exists at all outside of sleep. And yet I woke this morning surer than I have ever been of anything at court: that somewhere, she is real, and that I am already walking toward her.

She closed the book before she could read her own words back and doubt them. Then she rang for her attendants, and the Duchess of Albrecht became, once more, herself.

###CHAPTER 2: Roots · Morning at Castle Albrecht
Dawn came to Castle Albrecht the way it came to everything old and well-defended: slowly, and from a great height. The light found the eastern towers first, gilding the weathercocks before it ever reached the courtyard below, so that for a quarter of an hour each morning the castle seemed to wear a crown it took off again by breakfast.

In her chamber, Eleonore lay a while longer than she needed to, listening to the castle wake beneath her — the far-off clatter of the kitchens, a dog barking twice in the stable yard, the particular creak of the third step on the servants' stair that no amount of oil had ever silenced. These sounds were as familiar to her as her own pulse, and as little hers. She was mistress of all of it and author of none of it; the castle ran on rhythms laid down before her marriage and would run on them after, whatever she did or failed to do within its walls. She thought, not for the first time, that there was a particular loneliness in being loved by an entire household and known by none of it.

She broke her fast with Edward in the small dining hall he preferred to the great one. He did not look up from his letter. "You slept poorly. I heard you moving before the bell." Eleonore offered a small, practiced smile. "A dream, nothing more. It has already left me." He glanced up briefly. "See that it doesn't linger. You've the harvest accounts to review with the steward, and I'd have your opinion on them before I give mine to the King's man." She replied, "You always say you want my opinion before you've already decided what it will be." The ghost of a smile crossed his face, rare and real. "Because you are usually right, and it saves me the trouble of admitting it twice."

It was, Eleonore thought, as close as they came to tenderness most mornings — a kind of respect worn smooth and comfortable as an old glove, warm enough to live in, never quite warm enough to dream in. She did not resent it. She had married a fair man who kept his word, and she had seen enough of the alternative in other women's marriages to know what a mercy that was. But mercy, she had learned, and warmth were not always the same coin.

She left him to his letters and walked the long gallery toward the steward's office, and the castle moved around her the way a river moves around a stone that has always been there. She had seen the maps herself, once, spread across Edward's table in the candlelight while he thought she was asleep: the northern border marked in red ink where the marcher lords beyond the Greywater had grown bold, the King's demands for men and grain written in a hand grown increasingly impatient. War was a word no one said aloud in the halls of Albrecht, the way one does not say the name of an illness one hopes will pass if left unspoken. The world is arranging itself into something none of us chose, she thought, and did not yet know how true that thought would prove.

It was in search of air, more than duty, that she came out onto the south terrace an hour later. Below her, the gardens rolled away toward the orchard wall in their late-spring green, and it was here — only here — that the castle's weight seemed to lift from her shoulders. She was not looking for anything in particular when she saw the figure among the herb beds: a woman, dark-haired, sleeves pushed back, moving between the rows with an unhurried certainty that belonged entirely to herself, as though she answered to no bell and no household and no husband. Too far to call to. Too far to see her face clearly. And yet something in the set of her shoulders, in the private, listening way she paused over a plant before touching it, struck a chord in Eleonore that she could not name — like the first bars of a song she had once known by heart and had long since forgotten she knew.

She stood at the balustrade longer than she meant to, watching a stranger tend the earth, and felt, absurdly, as though she were seeing someone she had already met. It is the dream, she told herself, and did not believe it, and could not stop thinking of it either.

That night she took out her small book again. I saw a woman in the garden today, she wrote, tending the herbs, too far off to know her face. I do not know why I could not look away. I do not know why, of all the faces in this castle, hers is the one I carried up to my chamber with me, like a stone in my pocket I keep turning over. Perhaps it is nothing. Perhaps it is only that I dreamed of a shape in the mist, and my heart, foolish and hungry as it is, has decided to fit that shape to the first stranger who crossed my sight. And yet. She closed the book, blew out the candle, and lay a long while in the dark before sleep — and the woman who no longer walked ahead of her but had, at last, begun to turn around — came for her again.

###CHAPTER 3: Roots · The Valley of Women
Fifteen years earlier. A narrow valley half a kingdom south, where the river ran low and green between fields that had been sown by the same families for longer than anyone troubled to remember. The Aldermoss cottage sat at the valley's edge, its garden climbing the hillside in crooked, generous rows. It was harvest dusk, and the light had gone the colour of a ripe plum. A girl of twelve, dark-haired, barefoot, knelt in the dirt beside an old woman whose hands, though creased and spotted, moved among the roots with the certainty of someone reading a language only she knew.

"You're holding your breath again, child," the grandmother said, not looking up, sorting comfrey from nettle by touch alone. "Plants can feel a held breath. Makes them close up same as a hand you've frightened." Rowan let it out, half a laugh. "How does a root know if I'm breathing?" "It doesn't. You do. And what you know, your hands know, and what your hands know, the plant knows, sure as it knows the difference between rain and a bucket poured too fast."

She sat back on her heels, studying the girl with the particular fondness of someone counting the family's future in a face. "You've got the hands for it. Your mother had them. Her mother before that. Long as anyone's kept count, the women of this house have had them." Rowan said that Mira told her the village called them witch-women behind the hedgerow. Her grandmother tied off a bundle of comfrey, unbothered. "Let them call. They come quick enough when the fever takes a child, or when a woman's labour goes wrong past what their own mothers can manage. Fear and need make strange bedfellows, and we've long since stopped waiting on the first to catch up with the second."

Then she took Rowan's chin in one soil-dark hand, tilting her face up with a gentleness that belied the roughness of her palm. "I'll tell you a thing now, and you'll keep it the way you keep the seed count — close, and only for the right season to plant it. Your great-aunt Selin, who kept this garden before your mother did — she loved a woman. Loved her the whole of her life, the way some women love a man, and better than most I've seen love either. Elspeth, her name was. They kept house together forty years, right here, and when Selin died it was Elspeth's hands that closed her eyes."

Rowan sat very still. "No one ever speaks of her that way." "Not beyond this garden, no. But within it, always. Our power lives in two places, child: in the garden, and in the arms of the women we trust. Whichever kind of love finds you — a man's, a woman's, or none at all but the plants themselves — you'll not hear a word of shame from this family for it. That much I promise you, same as I promised your mother, and hers promised me."

Rowan turned the words over the way she turned a cutting before she planted it, searching for the place it would take root best. Above them, the first stars were coming out over the ridge, and somewhere down the valley a dog was barking at nothing. "I think I should like that," she said quietly. "To be loved like that. Steadily. Not hidden away, even if it's quiet." Her grandmother smiled and pressed a sprig of rosemary into her palm. "Then you will be, one day, if you keep your hands honest and your heart braver than your fear. That's the only charm I know that never fails."

The scene does not linger. It cannot — the valley that raised Rowan was not given the years it deserved. Within two winters, sickness came up from the river towns faster than any remedy could be gathered against it, and took the grandmother first, then an aunt, then, in the same terrible month, Rowan's mother. What the sickness spared, the following spring's flood carried off — the cottage, the garden terraces, the graves themselves half-swallowed by the swollen river before anyone could mourn them properly.

Rowan left the valley at sixteen with what fit in a single pack: a pouch of seeds saved from her mother's last harvest, a bundle of dried herbs wrapped in oilcloth, and a grief too large to carry any other way but forward. She walked north for the better part of a season, trading remedies for bread and a night's floor wherever the roads allowed, until she came, footsore and half-starved, to the gates of Castle Albrecht, where an aging healer named Mother Aveline took one look at the girl's hands — steady even after weeks of hunger — and did not ask her to leave.

"Whose teaching is this?" Aveline asked, examining a poultice Rowan had made unbidden for a stable-hand's burned arm. "My grandmother's," Rowan said. "And hers before her." Aveline set the poultice down with something like respect. "Then it seems your grandmother has just found herself an apprentice in this castle, whether either of us asked for one. Sleep in the stillroom tonight. We'll see what else those hands remember by morning."

Rowan stayed. She learned the castle's rhythms the way she had once learned the valley's, and when Aveline died some years later, quietly, in her sleep, with Rowan's hand in hers, the gardens and the stillroom passed to her without argument from anyone — for by then there was no one left in the castle who did not already trust her hands more than their own doubts. She became, in time, exactly what her grandmother had promised: a woman who answered to no bell but the seasons', who carried her whole lost lineage of love and healing quietly under her skin, waiting — though she did not yet know for what — to bloom again.

###CHAPTER 4: Roots · The Ornament of House Veyrac
Ten years before the story's opening. The library of House Veyrac, a manor grander in memory than in its current, thinning fortune. Past midnight. A single candle burned low over a desk buried in books that did not belong on a young lady's reading list — treatises on astrology, a cracked spine on the properties of rare herbs, a slim volume on alchemy borrowed without permission from her father's locked case. Eleonore, sixteen, sat curled in her nightgown, utterly absorbed.

The door opened without warning. Her mother stood in the frame. "Eleonore. It is past the second bell." Eleonore closed the book too quickly, guilt plain on her face. "I could not sleep. I thought reading might tire me." Her mother lifted the volume and read the spine with a sigh. "Alchemy. Of course it is alchemy, and not the psalter I left on your pillow." She set it down, not unkindly, and studied her daughter in the candlelight. "A lady's curiosity should never outpace her duty, Eleonore. I have told you this since you could walk."

"Why must the two be enemies, Mother?" Eleonore asked carefully. "I do my duty each day without complaint. Might I not also want to know how the world is made, in the hours no one requires anything of me?" Her mother was quiet a moment, and something crossed her face that was not quite disapproval — closer to a memory of her own, long since folded away. "Because the world does not ask what a woman wants to know. It asks what she is worth, and to this house, just now, you are worth a great deal. Our name is old and our coffers are not what they were. You are our best asset and our dearest daughter both, and I would have you understand that those two things are not always easy to hold in one hand." She touched Eleonore's cheek, briefly, gently. "Hide your books better, if you must keep them. But hide them, child. For your own sake as much as the house's."

She took the candle and went, leaving Eleonore alone with the guttering flame and the forbidden volume, which she pressed a dried sprig of lavender between its pages, and returned to reading until the wick drowned in its own wax.

The bargain her mother spoke of came due within the year. Eleonore met the Duke of Albrecht at a ball arranged, with great and visible relief, by her parents — a solid, dependable man whose alliance would steady House Veyrac's finances for a generation. She danced with him twice, answered his careful questions with careful answers, and watched her mother's face across the ballroom soften with something close to gratitude. Behind her smile, Eleonore told herself quietly: If I must play this role, then I will shape it to my own design.

She married him within the year. Life in the castle gilded her cage exactly as she had expected — she was admired, envied, gossiped about in equal measure, and some of the court's sharper eyes suspected, not wrongly, that a woman so serene must be hiding something beneath the calm. They imagined ambition. They did not imagine a mind that still hungered after alchemy and astrology by candlelight, or a heart that had not yet been asked a single question it wanted to answer honestly. Among the herb beds and hidden pages of her books, Eleonore kept her rebellion small, quiet, and entirely her own — waiting, the way a seed waits beneath frost for a spring it has not yet been promised.

###CHAPTER 5: Roots · The Duke and His Son
Eight years before the story's opening. The training yard at Castle Albrecht, early morning, frost still silvering the ground. Duke Edward, forty and iron-backed, stood watching his son — Henry, seventeen, quick and restless — spar against the arms-master with more enthusiasm than discipline. Henry lost his footing, went down hard, and came up laughing before he came up angry.

"Again," Edward said, not raising his voice, which was somehow worse than if he had. "And this time watch his shoulder, not his blade. A blade only tells you where he has already decided to be." Henry picked himself up, wiping mud from his jaw. "I watched his shoulder. His shoulder lied to me." "Then you have learned something true about men this morning, which is worth more than any bout you might have won."

They sparred a short round themselves, more ritual than contest — Edward's economy of movement against Henry's restless energy — until Edward disarmed him with a turn of the wrist so unhurried it looked almost gentle. Henry stood a moment, breathing hard, looking at his empty hand. "You never tell me I've done well," he said, quieter now. "Only that I might, someday, if I temper this or watch that."

Edward was silent a long moment, and when he spoke there was something almost unpracticed in his voice, which did not come easily to him. "I watched your mother die believing she had never once heard me say I loved her, though God knows I did, every day of our marriage, in every way but the one she wanted spoken aloud. I have tried, since, not to make the same silence twice. If I fail at it with you, Henry, know that the failure is old habit, not absence of feeling. You do well. You have always done well. I would simply have you do well and survive whatever the world sends north to test you, and the second requires more patience than either of us finds easy to come by."

It was as close as Edward ever came, in Henry's hearing, to saying the words outright. Henry said nothing for a moment — surprised into silence by an honesty so unlike his father's usual reserve — then nodded, once, and picked his blade back up off the frost-hard ground. "Again, then. And this time I'll watch his shoulder and yours both."

Edward's approval, when it came at all, came sparingly, in a currency so rarely spent that Henry treasured even its smallest coin. It shaped him, in the years that followed, into a man restless still, curious about the world beyond the castle walls in a way his father's steadiness had never satisfied, and never would.

###CHAPTER 6: Year One · Spring — First Awakening
It was some days after that first distant glimpse from the terrace that Eleonore found an excuse to walk in the gardens herself, telling herself it was only for the air.

The castle gardens after a morning rain. The clouds were breaking, shafts of pale sunlight falling on beds of green. The air was heavy with the scent of wet earth, rosemary, and lavender. Rowan was kneeling, her apron damp, hands stained with soil, humming softly under her breath — a tune from her childhood. Eleonore appeared at the archway, unseen at first, and paused, watching.

"How peaceful it looks here," Eleonore said softly, almost to herself. "As though the world beyond these walls does not exist." Rowan looked up at the sound of her voice, her expression calm but guarded. A few strands of hair had fallen loose against her cheek. She wiped her hands on her apron as Eleonore stepped closer. "My lady. The paths are still wet — you'll soil your hem." Eleonore smiled faintly. "Let them. It is worth the risk, if it brings me here."

Rowan arched a brow but said nothing, returning to the herb bed. Eleonore knelt beside her without hesitation, gathering her skirts. The damp earth seeped cold through the fine fabric, but she did not flinch. Instead, she dipped her fingers into the soil, curious. "It feels alive. As though it breathes beneath my touch." Rowan glanced at her, voice quieter. "You notice what most do not." There was admiration in her tone, though carefully hidden.

The silence between them thickened with unspoken things. Rowan reached for a sprig of thyme, breaking it gently. "Here. Rub the leaves between your fingers." Eleonore did so, and the sharp green scent burst into the air. She inhaled, eyes closing briefly in delight. Rowan watched her more than the plant. "How miraculous," Eleonore said with a soft laugh, "that such a small thing can hold so much fragrance. It feels almost like… a secret." "Perhaps it is," Rowan said.

Eleonore's hand lingered near Rowan's, their fingers nearly touching. She did not pull away. Instead, her voice dropped lower, almost confessional. "Do you ever feel, Rowan, that you are meant for secrets? That you walk through the world carrying something no one else can see?" Rowan's breath caught. For a heartbeat, she considered the truth of her lineage, her whispered traditions, the quiet magic she carried. But she only offered a careful smile. "Some things cannot be spoken in halls of stone. The earth keeps them safe until the right soul listens."

Their eyes met again, and this time neither looked away at once. The garden hummed with life around them: bees in the rosemary, the call of a dove, the drip of water from the eaves. "Then perhaps," Eleonore said, barely above a whisper, "I wish to listen."

For a long moment, Rowan's roughened hand remained beneath Eleonore's, soil-stained yet warm. The air stilled around them, as if even the garden held its breath. But the truth pressed between them — unspoken, trembling — was too large for that morning. Rowan's fingers tightened once, a fleeting promise, before she let go, turning back to the soil, movements careful and deliberate, as if grounding herself in the work. Eleonore watched her in silence, heart unsteady, her own hand resting lightly in her lap. She did not wipe the soil away. Instead, she cherished the trace of contact — fragile proof that something had begun.

Later that afternoon, Eleonore withdrew to her chamber. The rain had begun again. She set aside her embroidery untouched, her hands still faintly stained from the soil. A trace of thyme lingered on her skin. She brought her fingers to her lips, as if to catch the memory. "I wished to listen… and she heard me," she whispered. She rose and walked to the mirror. There was a smudge of earth at the edge of her sleeve, dark against silk. Normally she would summon a maid to tidy her appearance, but she left it as it was — proof that she had been there, with Rowan, her hands in the soil like any common woman. How strange, she thought, that the memory of a touch can weigh more heavily than a room full of courtly compliments. The seed had taken root in her chest. She knew it — though she could not yet name what it would grow to become.

###CHAPTER 7: Year One · Summer — Blossoming Connection
A blazing midsummer afternoon. The gardens hummed with bees, the air thick with the scent of roses and sage. Rowan worked beneath a shaded arbor, cutting herbs and tying them into bundles. Eleonore entered with a parasol, though she soon closed it, preferring the dappled sunlight that filtered through the vines.

"Every time I come, I find you bent over another basket of leaves," Eleonore said, playful. "Do you never tire of green?" Rowan looked up, sweat glistening faintly at her temple, but her hands did not pause. "Green does not tire of me, my lady. It clings to me, whether I wish it or not." Eleonore laughed — a soft musical sound that made Rowan glance at her again, longer this time. Encouraged, Eleonore stepped closer, trailing her fingers across a rosemary bush. "This place feels different when you are in it. Alive. Almost enchanted."

Rowan shrugged, though her eyes flickered with something deeper. "Enchantment is only patience and listening. Plants grow the way hearts beat — whether watched or not." Eleonore lowered her voice, teasing. "Then tell me… what does my heart reveal, when you listen?" The question hung between them, bold as sunlight. Rowan stilled, the herb bundle half-tied in her hands. "More than I should dare to say." Their eyes locked. A servant's laughter carried faintly from the distant orchard, breaking the spell. Rowan looked back to her work, fingers tightening the knot.

"You speak as though words are dangerous," Eleonore said, gently, after a pause. "Sometimes they are," Rowan replied, low. Silence fell, but it was not empty. Eleonore slowly sat on the bench beside Rowan's basket, folding her hands neatly in her lap. For once she did not fill the air with chatter — she only sat near, breathing in the scents of crushed herbs and summer heat.

"When I was a child, I dreamed of secrets," she said, soft and honest, after a long silence. "Of hidden worlds behind locked doors. Now I think perhaps the hidden things were never far away at all… only waiting for me to open my eyes." Rowan looked at her, struck by the honesty in her tone. For a heartbeat, she almost reached for Eleonore's hand — but instead pressed another sprig of rosemary into her palm. "Then keep this. A reminder. Sometimes the smallest things carry the deepest truths." Eleonore closed her hand around it, her fingers brushing Rowan's as she took it. The warmth between them was heavy as the summer air — not spoken, not declared, but undeniable.

The afternoon heat lingered even within the cool stone halls as Eleonore returned to her chambers, a faint flush on her cheeks. "Words too dangerous…" she whispered to herself. "And yet… what danger could I wish more than silence?" She moved to her writing desk and tucked the rosemary sprig between the pages of her private notebook. For a moment she hovered, wondering if she dared to write the name — Rowan — in her own hand. She did not. Not yet. Outside her window, the summer air hummed with cicadas. In her chest, something hummed too — a secret rhythm, wild and new.

###CHAPTER 8: Year One · Autumn — Deepening Intimacy
Late afternoon in early autumn. The castle garden glowed with amber light. Leaves drifted down from the orchard trees, and the last blooms of the season nodded heavy with seed. Rowan knelt near a low stone wall, gathering dried herbs into baskets for winter storage. Eleonore entered, wearing a cloak lined with soft fur, her hair braided against the breeze.

"Summer has left you with a great harvest," she said, smiling softly. "It's as if you've trapped the season in your baskets." Rowan looked up, her fingers dusty with crushed lavender. "Only trying to keep what might be needed later. Winter spares nothing." Eleonore knelt slightly beside her, reaching to touch a dried blossom. "And yet you coax life from the earth even as it fades. You make endings beautiful."

Rowan glanced at her, startled by the intimacy of the words. Eleonore's cloak brushed the soil as she crouched closer. Rowan's fingers stilled, trembling faintly as she tied off the bundle of herbs. "You speak as though I had power over such things. I only tend what's given." Eleonore whispered, "And do you not see what you've given me?"

Silence. The words hung between them like a leaf caught mid-fall. Rowan swallowed hard, looking down at her hands. Her voice, when it came, was low and rough. "My lady… you mustn't." "I must," Eleonore said, gently. "Or else my heart will wither before the frost."

A gust of wind scattered leaves around them. One landed on Rowan's shoulder; Eleonore reached out to brush it away. Her fingers grazed the curve of Rowan's neck — not an accident this time, but a soft, deliberate stroke. Rowan closed her eyes for a heartbeat, breathing in sharply, before taking Eleonore's hand and holding it — not pulling away, not yet. "You don't know what you risk," Rowan whispered. "Perhaps I do," Eleonore said firmly, though her voice shook. "And perhaps I would risk more."

Rowan opened her eyes. In them, Eleonore saw both fear and yearning — an entire life of restraint trembling at the edge of surrender. But Rowan let her hand remain, clasped in Eleonore's. A bell tolled from the castle tower, distant and mournful. They did not move.

The garden in autumn is a place of endings, but also of seeds hidden in the earth, waiting for spring. In the clasp of their hands, something fragile and fierce began to root.

Back in her chamber, the light dimmer now, the sun setting earlier, Eleonore leaned against the closed door, her cloak still on, her pulse still running from the garden. "Rowan…" she whispered, taking the name as if it were a prayer. It was not like the stolen glances of court, she thought. It was steady. Frightened. Fierce. The kind of touch that does not seduce but anchors. The kind that tells you that you are seen. She opened the small book of pressed herbs and added a dried lavender sprig alongside the rosemary from the summer before. "Two seasons," she murmured. "Two tokens." I thought I understood longing, she thought. I did not. Not until her.

###CHAPTER 9: Year One · The Steward's Eye
Godfrey had served House Albrecht for thirty years, first as the old duke's page and now as steward of the household, and in all that time he had prided himself on missing nothing. It was Godfrey who noticed that the duchess's evening walks had grown longer, that she returned from the gardens with color in her cheeks that no exertion with a trowel could account for, and that the gardener Rowan — a woman of no family, no dowry, no claim to anyone's attention — was spoken of in the kitchens with an odd, protective fondness, as though the servants shared a secret they were keeping from him.

He said nothing for a long while, turning it over as he turned over the household accounts, patiently, suspiciously. Then, one grey afternoon, he found an excuse to corner Rowan in the stillroom, asking pointed questions about which hours she kept and for whom. "The duchess visits you often, I'm told," he said, mild but watchful. "Later than a lady ought to be out among the beds, some evenings." Rowan did not look up from her mortar. "Her Grace has an interest in remedies. I answer what she asks." "Mm. And does Her Grace often ask her questions after the household is abed?"

Before Rowan could answer, the door opened and Henry stepped in, unhurried, as though he had been looking for exactly this room. "Godfrey. Good — you can settle an argument. I've told my father his physician bleeds him too often for his headaches, and that Rowan's willow-bark tonic does more good than a lancet. I've been coming to her myself these past weeks. Ask her yourself what hour I generally trouble her — you'll find it's later than is polite, and entirely my own fault." Godfrey looked between them, his suspicion visibly wrong-footed by so plain and plausible an explanation from the heir himself. "I meant no accusation, my lord. Only the household's good order." Henry clapped him lightly on the shoulder, already steering him toward the door. "Of course. No one keeps better order than you, Godfrey. Walk with me — I want your opinion on the east wall repairs."

He glanced back once at Rowan as he went, the smallest nod passing between them: safe, for now. When the door closed behind them, Rowan set down her mortar and found that her hands were not quite steady.

Henry told himself, walking Godfrey firmly toward the east wall, that he had done it only for the household's peace. He did not examine too closely why the thought of the duchess named in gossip had put such an edge in his voice, nor why some part of him had wanted, quite badly, to be the one who kept her safe.

###CHAPTER 10: Year One · Winter — The Turning Point
A snow-laden evening. The garden lay silent under a soft white blanket, its paths unmarked except for two sets of footprints leading to the glasshouse at its edge. Inside, the air was warmer, scented with damp earth and the faint perfume of overwintering herbs. Frost clung to the panes, blurring the world beyond.

Rowan stood inside the glasshouse, lighting a small oil lamp. The glow softened the shadows of her face. The door creaked; Eleonore slipped in, wrapped in a heavy cloak, her breath misting in the chill. She closed the door quietly behind her. "I thought I might find you here," she said, hushed. Rowan turned, startled but softening. "You should be by the fire, my lady. It's bitter tonight." Eleonore smiled faintly. "Perhaps. But there's warmth enough here."

Rowan looked at her — at the snow melting in her hair, at the pale glow of her face in the lamplight. Eleonore moved closer, the hush of the glasshouse making every sound more intimate. "You risk much, coming here after dark," Rowan said quietly. "I am tired of risking only in my thoughts," Eleonore replied, firmly, though her voice trembled.

Rowan exhaled, her breath shuddering. She set the lamp on a low table among the potted herbs. For a long moment they simply looked at each other — the silence between them full of everything they had not said. "I tried to keep this safe," Rowan said, low, almost breaking. "I tried to keep you safe." Eleonore stepped closer. "And yet here we are."

She lifted her hands and slowly lowered her hood. Her hair spilled free, pale and soft against the dark of her cloak. Rowan's eyes closed briefly, as if the sight were too much. When she opened them, Eleonore was standing only a breath away. "Do you think me so fragile?" Eleonore whispered. "That my heart cannot bear the truth?"

Rowan trembled; her hands hovered between them, unsure whether to reach or retreat. Eleonore took them gently, turning the calloused palms upward, pressing them to her own heart through the heavy cloak. "Here. Feel it. It beats for you."

For a heartbeat, Rowan did not move. Then she closed her fingers over Eleonore's, holding on as if anchoring herself. The lamp flickered; a petal drifted from a wintering plant and landed on the table between them. "If this is a sin, I cannot repent it," Rowan whispered, broken. Eleonore leaned closer, her forehead against Rowan's. "Then let it be no sin. Let it be what it is."

They stood there in the lamplight, foreheads touching, hands entwined, the scent of damp earth and winter herbs wrapping around them. No kiss yet — only the surety of two hearts meeting at last without disguise. Outside, snow began to fall harder, sealing the world beyond in silence.

The corridors of the castle were silent, muffled by the snow, as Eleonore climbed the stairs. Her cloak was still heavy with cold, but beneath it, her skin was warm where Rowan's hands had pressed. Her hands were trembling, she thought. She opened her notebook and added a third token: a tiny white petal fallen from the wintering plant in the glasshouse. "Three seasons," she said softly. "Three tokens. How can I hide what has already rooted in me?" If winter strips everything bare, she thought, then let it strip me too. I will stand as I am — unadorned, unmasked — and wait for the spring she has promised me with her eyes.

###CHAPTER 11: Year One · Spring Returns — Blossoming into Love
A soft, misty morning. The garden, once bare, was now pale green with new shoots and buds. The glasshouse stood open, windows propped to let in the fresh air. Rowan knelt by a bed of early flowers, planting seedlings with careful hands. Eleonore walked toward her without her cloak, her gown simple, her hair loose around her shoulders. There was no one else about — the castle was still at breakfast.

Rowan looked up as Eleonore approached, brushing soil from her palms. Her eyes widened slightly at the sight of the duchess unadorned, sunlight catching her hair. Eleonore knelt beside her without hesitation, the damp earth soaking the hem of her dress. "Another spring," she said, smiling softly. "And yet everything feels… new." Rowan searched her face. "Some things survive the winter stronger than before."

Eleonore reached for a seedling and helped press it into the soil. Their hands met over the little plant, fingers curling into each other's. This time neither of them pulled away. "I have no fear left, Rowan," Eleonore said, low and certain. Rowan trembled, her lips parting as though to speak, but no words came. Eleonore shifted closer, their knees touching in the damp soil. "My lady…" Rowan whispered. "Not here. Not now. Not 'my lady,'" Eleonore said, firm and tender at once.

Rowan's breath caught. Slowly, she lifted a hand to Eleonore's face, brushing a stray wisp of hair from her cheek. Eleonore leaned into the touch. "Eleonore…" Rowan said, softly, almost in disbelief. The sound of her name in Rowan's mouth made Eleonore close her eyes. She leaned forward, their foreheads touching as before — but now she tilted her face, and at last their lips met, a hesitant, trembling kiss that deepened as their hands clutched at each other in the soil.

The garden was no longer a place of secrecy. It was a sanctuary, a beginning. The seed planted in spring, hidden through summer, ripened in autumn, and sheltered through winter now flowered between them — bright, fearless, alive. They drew back slightly, breathless but smiling. Eleonore's fingers stayed entwined with Rowan's, soil smudging their skin. Neither of them cared.

"This is what I prayed for in silence," Eleonore whispered, smiling through tears. Rowan smiled back, her eyes shining. "And this is what I kept safe for you." The morning mist lifted; the sun spilled gold over the new growth. In its light, two women knelt side by side, no longer duchess and servant, planting the first flowers of a life they would build together.

How could something so simple, Eleonore thought later — soil beneath them, seedlings trembling in their beds — hold so much power? She had been afraid once. Afraid of whispers, of duty, of the weight of titles pressing down like a stone. But kneeling in the dirt with Rowan's hand warm in hers, she had felt no chains at all. There is no courtly mask in her, she thought. No hollow flattery. She sees me — not as a duchess, not as a wife bound by alliance, but as Eleonore. As a woman whose heart still dares to bloom.

And Rowan, walking back to the stillroom, still felt the warmth in her palms. Her grandmother had been right. The soil was still under her nails. The kiss had not been the soft, timid thing she had imagined in her sleepless nights; it had been steady, certain, like the push of a seed through soil. I have spent my life coaxing green things to grow in silence, she thought. Now something green and wild grows in me, and I will tend it with all the care I have ever given a garden. Spring has returned to the garden. But this time, it has returned to me as well.

###CHAPTER 12: Year One · A Secret Night in the Garden
The castle slept. Its stone corridors echoed only with the wind, while the garden beyond lay hushed in silver moonlight. The glasshouse glowed faintly, its panes catching the stars.

Eleonore slipped from the shadow of the great hall, her cloak held tight. Her heart thudded as she crossed the damp grass, for she knew whom she would find waiting. And there she was — Rowan, standing among the herbs, her dark hair gleaming like wet silk, her green eyes catching every trace of starlight.

Neither spoke at first. Eleonore reached out with trembling fingers, brushing Rowan's cheek. Rowan caught her hand and pressed it to her lips, closing her eyes as if the gesture itself were prayer. "Eleonore," Rowan whispered, the name breaking like water.

The Duchess could not answer in words. She pulled Rowan close, their mouths meeting in a kiss that was no longer hesitant or searching, but deep, certain. Rowan's hands moved to her waist, while Eleonore's fingers slid into the dark braid, loosening it until strands fell across Rowan's shoulders. The air between them warmed with the rush of breath and the brush of cloth. Eleonore's cloak slipped to the ground unnoticed. They stumbled together against the wooden bench, laughter breaking between kisses, soft and breathless. Their touches grew slower then, more deliberate, the air thick with a tension that was no longer innocent but irresistible. Foreheads touched, hands lingered. Every movement spoke what neither dared to name aloud.

And then — the garden kept its secrets.

The night folded around them, the moon drawing a veil of silver over glass and leaves. What passed between them beyond that veil belonged to them alone: a union of trust, of hunger, of love that had waited through four seasons and now finally bloomed.

###CHAPTER 13: Year Two · The King's Summons
The messenger came in early autumn of the second year, his horse blown, his King's colors dark with road-dust. He was shown to Edward's study within the hour, and by evening the whole castle knew, in the particular way castles always know things before they are told: the hill lords beyond the Greywater had broken the old truce, burned two border villages, and put the King's garrison at Thornhold to the sword. Edward was summoned north — not merely to send men, as he had in past seasons, but to go himself, sword and seal both, and treat with what remained of the King's authority in that country.

He told Eleonore himself, that evening, in the small dining hall where they had shared so many quiet, dutiful meals. "I leave within the week. Henry rides with me as far as the river; the King wishes him seen, so the border lords know the succession is settled and secure. You will govern the household in my absence, as you have done before." Eleonore asked, quietly, "How long?" "As long as it takes to make men behave who have decided not to." A pause, and something almost gentle crossed his face. "You need not fear for the estate. You have run it as well as I have these many winters, whether either of us has said so aloud." Eleonore was surprised into honesty. "You have never said so." A rare, rueful almost-smile. "No. I find I am saying a great many things this week I have left too long unsaid. It is what leaving does to a man, I think — makes him suddenly generous with a currency he was always too proud to spend."

It was, in eleven years of marriage, the nearest Edward ever came to telling her he loved her, and though it fell short of the word itself, Eleonore would remember it, later, as a kindness she had not expected and did not deserve to have expected so little.

He left three days later at the head of a modest column, Henry riding beside him in his father's colors, and Eleonore watched from the gatehouse until the road took them from sight, feeling, against all her own better judgment, a genuine fear for a man she had never managed to love and had somehow, over eleven quiet years, come to need.

###CHAPTER 14: Year Two · News from the Border
The gatehouse of Castle Albrecht, five weeks later. A raw, wet dusk. Eleonore stood at the window of the steward's tower, a folded letter — Henry's hand, hurried, the ink blotted twice — still open in her hand. She had read it four times already and did not need to read it a fifth; she only could not stop. "He says his father lives," she told Godfrey, who hovered uncertainly at the door. "He says they are bringing him home. He does not say how badly, and I think that not saying is its own answer."

On the third morning after the letter came, a rider arrived ahead of the column to warn the gate, and by midday the whole household had gathered in the yard to watch the litter come through at a walker's pace, drawn by two horses led carefully around every rut in the road. Henry walked beside it, one hand on the litter's rail the entire way, his face grey with three days' exhaustion and something harder than exhaustion beneath it.

Eleonore was already kneeling beside the litter before anyone thought to stop her, duchess's skirts forgotten in the mud. Edward's face was slack, his color wrong, a linen wrapping gone brown at the edge above his temple. His eyes did not open. His breath came shallow but steady. Eleonore looked up at Henry. "Tell me plainly. Not the kind version — the true one." His voice was hoarse from three days of holding himself together. "A hill-lord's mace, in the last skirmish before they'd sue for terms. He was still breathing when they pulled him clear, and he has not stopped since. The physicians said what physicians always say when they don't know — that the head is a strange country, that some men wake in a day and some in a season and some never." His composure cracked, just slightly, just for her. "I could not lose him on that road, Eleonore. I would not let myself."

She gripped his hand once, hard, before turning to the household physician. "Do what you know," she said. "But I will tell you honestly — if your knowing has limits, send for Rowan. Her remedies have outlasted yours before, and I will not have this castle's stubbornness be the reason we did not try everything."

It was the physician himself who asked it, out of plain professional respect for a healer whose skill he had quietly envied for years. Rowan came within the quarter hour, her hands already full of what she judged she would need, and did not once look toward Eleonore as she knelt at the Duke's bedside — a mercy of its own, though neither of them named it as such. She worked through that first night and many after: cooling compresses laid and changed by the hour, the bitter tinctures her grandmother had taught her for fevers of the blood and the brain, a patience that outlasted the physicians' own.

She never once, in all the long weeks that followed, said aloud what it cost her to tend, with steady and skillful hands, the recovery of the one man whose death, however she would never have wished it, would have made her own life simpler. Eleonore, watching her from the doorway one evening, understood the gift being given her without a word passing between them, and loved Rowan, in that moment, more than she had in a year of gentler ones.

Edward did not wake. He breathed, and his color slowly returned, and his heart beat on with the same stubborn steadiness he had brought to every other duty of his life — but his eyes did not open, and the castle settled, uneasily, into the long grey work of waiting.

###CHAPTER 15: Year Two · What Henry Saw
Henry took up the burden of the household the way he took up everything: too fast, and with more feeling than his father would ever have shown, snapping at stewards one day and weeping alone in the chapel the next, though he let no one see the second. He had come home from the border a harder man than he had left it, and grief and fear together had worn away whatever boyishness had once let him pretend his feelings for Eleonore were simple.

He saw her now as a regent sees a duchess: capable, composed, holding the estate together through a crisis that would have broken lesser women, and it was this — her strength, more than her beauty — that finally undid the drawer in his heart he had kept so carefully shut.

It was a raw evening in early winter when he came upon the glasshouse by chance, meaning only to walk off an hour he could not spend usefully any other way, and saw through the fogged glass two women in the lamplight, and one of them was laughing — laughing, fully and without performance, in a way he had never once seen from her at his father's table in eleven years — with her hand held inside the gardener's, their heads bent close. He did not go in. He did not make a sound. He stood in the cold a long moment, understanding arriving in him slow and total, like water finding every crack in a wall at once, and then he walked back to the castle alone, and told no one, and did not sleep that night, nor for several after.

Lord Chancellor Bertrand arrived from the capital before the first snow, sent by the King to see the border settlement through and, more delicately, to see the succession of Albrecht settled alongside it. He was an old man, dry as parchment, and entirely without malice, which made his counsel harder to resist than cruelty would have been. "Your father may wake tomorrow, or in ten years, or never, my lord, and the King's peace cannot wait on a physician's guess," he told Henry in the map room, not unkindly. "The duchess's dower lands and the Veyrac alliance pass with her — to whatever house she next marries into, should your father not recover. The King would rest easier if that alliance remained where it has always served the realm best: within these walls. She is a fine woman, my lord. And you are unmarried. I say only what every man from here to the capital is already thinking."

Henry said nothing for a long while, looking down at the map of a border he had bled to hold, and did not tell the Chancellor what he had seen through a glasshouse window, nor why the suggestion now sat in his chest like a stone. The Chancellor found occasion, some days later, to raise the same matter with Eleonore herself, and she heard the proposal plainly enough beneath the courtesy, and gave him no answer beyond a duchess's practiced grace, and carried the weight of it to her chamber alone that night.

###CHAPTER 16: Year Two · Eleonore's Refusal
He came to her a week later, in the small dining hall where she had once eaten so many quiet breakfasts with his father, and stood a long while before he could make himself begin. "I am not going to pretend the Chancellor's counsel is only the King's," Henry said. "I have thought of little else for a week, and I find I cannot make myself sorry for thinking it, whatever else is true. Eleonore — if my father does not wake, the realm will want this settled, and I find I do not only want it settled. I have wanted you, I think, longer than I've let myself say so, even to myself."

Eleonore sat very still, her hands folded in her lap, her face giving nothing away that he could read. "Your father is not dead, Henry." "No. And I would have him wake tomorrow and never think of this again, whatever that costs me. I am not asking you to wish him gone. I am asking you to know, when the day comes that this question can no longer be put off, that there is an answer already waiting that would cost you nothing — no scandal, no loss, only what the whole castle already half expects."

He did not say, though it stood plainly in his face, that he already knew what it would truly cost her, and had come anyway, hoping against everything he had seen through that fogged glass that hope might yet be permitted him.

Eleonore answered after a long silence, gently but without a single tremor. "You are a good man, Henry, and you deserve better than to have your heart handed back to you the way I must now hand it. But I will not marry you — not if your father wakes tomorrow, and not if he never wakes at all."

Henry asked, quiet, already knowing, needing to hear it said: "Because of her." It was not truly a question, and Eleonore did not treat it as one. She met his eyes and answered with the same quiet steadiness that had carried her through every crisis this long year had asked of her. "Because of her. I did not choose it to wound anyone, least of all you, and I am sorry — more sorry than I can say — that you saw what you were never meant to carry alone. But I will not trade one bargain of alliance for another, Henry, not even a kinder one, not even yours. I have spent eleven years learning to live well within a marriage I did not choose. I find, now that I finally know what it is to be chosen freely instead, that I cannot go back to being merely useful."

Henry looked at her a long moment, something in his face breaking and, slowly, mending itself into something steadier. "I saw you both. In the glasshouse, in the winter before last. I told myself for a long time it meant less than it did." "And now?" Eleonore asked, softly, unflinching. Something in him almost, not quite, laughed. "Now I think I have spent a great deal of grief on a woman who was never going to be unhappy without me. There is a certain mercy in that, once the sting of it passes." He straightened, some old discipline of his father's surfacing in him at last. "I will tell the Chancellor the matter is closed. I will not tell him why, and I will not tell him what I saw, not now, not ever — you have my word on that, whatever else you think of me tonight."

"I think a great deal of you tonight, Henry," Eleonore said quietly. "More than I did this morning." He bowed to her, formal as a stranger and yet gentler than he had ever been with her, and left her alone in the small dining hall, where eleven years of quiet breakfasts had finally given way to something neither dutiful nor bargained — a kindness, freely given, from a man who had wanted, for a while, to be loved instead of merely spared.

###CHAPTER 17: Year Two · Edward's Final Grace
Edward woke, in the end, on a grey morning in early spring — not fully, not for long, but enough. His eyes opened; his hand, when Eleonore took it, closed weakly around hers; and for perhaps an hour, before the fever that had lain coiled beneath his long sleep finally rose up to claim what the wound had not, he was entirely himself.

He said little. He asked after the harvest, and after Henry, and seemed satisfied, in his quiet way, with both answers. Then, holding her hand still, he looked at her with an attention she had not felt from him in all their years together, as though duty had finally worn thin enough to let something else show through. "You have done well by this house, Eleonore," he said, weak but clear. "Better than I ever told you, which was my failing, not yours." A pause, gathering the last of his strength. "Whatever comes after me — let it be settled plainly. Henry inherits outright, my seal on it, no conditions laid on you. You have carried enough of other people's bargains. I'd not have my death be one more."

Eleonore, unable to speak, pressed his hand to her lips. He seemed, in the last clear moment his eyes were open, almost at peace with what he saw there — not the passion he had never given her and never asked for in return, but something he perhaps valued more, at the very end: a debt, quietly and fully forgiven.

He did not wake again. He died four days later, in his own bed, with Henry on one side of him and Eleonore on the other, and the household mourned him honestly, for whatever he had lacked in warmth he had never once lacked in fairness, and a castle remembers fairness longer than it remembers fire.

His dying word, entered into the official record by the Chancellor himself before he rode south again, settled every question of succession the border crisis had raised: Henry, Duke of Albrecht, unconditionally — and the dowager duchess Eleonore, free to order her own life as she saw fit, with the full honor and provision due her position, for as long as she lived.

No one asked her, after that, why she never remarried. It was assumed, quietly and universally, to be grief — and Eleonore, who had grieved Edward honestly, if not the way the household imagined, never troubled to correct them.

###CHAPTER 18: After — The New Duke
Henry ruled well, in time — better, some said, than his father, for he governed with his father's fairness and a warmth Edward had never quite managed. He did not marry for some years, and when he did, it was a match his council was pleased with and his own heart had grown calm enough to welcome without complication. He was, by all accounts, a contented man, and if some old ember of what he had once felt for his stepmother never entirely went out, he tended it the way one tends the memory of a fever survived — with respect, and no wish at all to have it back.

He did not become Rowan and Eleonore's confidant, not in the easy way of old friends; there remained, always, a careful formality between them, the courtesy of a man who has made his peace with a wound rather than forgotten it. But he was fair, and he was grateful — to Eleonore for a mercy she had never framed as one, and to Rowan, in his own private accounting, for a healer's hands that had bought his father four more days of life than the border had any right to allow — and fairness and gratitude, tended patiently enough, grow eventually into something very near to respect.

It was several years into his reign, well after any grief had softened into ordinary memory, that he summoned Rowan to the hall and, with rather more ceremony than the moment strictly required, granted her the title of Keeper of the Gardens and the Healing House, with lodgings of her own choosing anywhere on the estate. Rowan searched his face. "You did not have to make it formal, my lord." Henry offered a small, hard-won smile. "No. But a household runs easier on what is written than on what is merely understood, and I find, these days, that I would rather my stepmother's happiness rest on something sturdier than my own good will. Wills change. Deeds do not, once sealed."

It was not friendship, quite. But it was, in its own careful way, a kind of love — the kind a man learns to give once he has stopped needing to be loved back in the particular shape he once wanted.

###CHAPTER 19: Epilogue — A Garden in Full Bloom
Many years later, the dower house at the edge of the estate was known simply as the place where the dowager duchess and her companion kept their garden — the finest in the dukedom, visitors said, though none could quite say why two women's care should make it so.

Eleonore, her hair silvered now but her eyes as quick as ever, still walked the paths each morning before the household woke. Rowan, her hands still stained with soil though her back had grown less willing to kneel as easily as it once had, still hummed the old tune from her childhood over the herb beds. Their notebook of pressed flowers — rosemary, lavender, a single white petal, and dozens of tokens added in all the years since — sat on the shelf between their two chairs by the fire, worn soft at the corners from handling.

Duke Henry visited once or twice a season, always announced, always correct, and always, in his own reserved way, glad to come — bringing news of the realm, and once, memorably, his own small daughter, who took one look at the garden and refused to leave it until dark. No one in three generations of the household ever spoke the word scandal in connection with the dower house, nor needed to; a duke's quiet approval, once given, settles a matter more thoroughly than any denial ever could.

Eleonore never remarried, and the household, which had once wondered, stopped wondering long before she stopped being asked. She had, everyone agreed, given her heart entirely to her late husband's memory — and if the shape that devotion took each evening was a woman's hand in hers before the fire, well, grief was a private country, and no one thought it their place to draw its borders for her.

On quiet evenings, when the light went gold over the beds and the bees grew slow and heavy in the last of the summer roses, Eleonore would take Rowan's hand exactly as she had that first spring morning, soil and all, and Rowan would say, exactly as her grandmother once said to her, that their power lived in two places: in the garden, and in the arms of the women they trusted.

The line of women had not ended with Rowan. It had only found new soil, and taken root, and bloomed.
